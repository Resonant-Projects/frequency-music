# The Tuning Codec: Temperament as Lossy Compression

_Every tuning system is a codec. Every codec is a tuning system. The parallel isn't metaphorical — it's structural._

---

## Two Problems, One Shape

In "The Comma Problem," we explored how tuning systems distribute an irreducible mathematical error — the gap between pure intervals and closed pitch cycles. In "The Codec Ear," we explored how neural audio compressors discover, through training, which features of sound the ear actually tracks. These essays felt like neighbors. It turns out they're the same house, seen from different streets.

The core problem in both cases: **you have an infinite, high-dimensional space of possibilities, and you need to represent it with a finite, low-dimensional code.** The space of all possible frequency ratios is the rationals (actually, a free abelian group generated by the primes — the so-called "harmonic lattice"). The space of all possible audio signals is a continuous high-dimensional waveform space. In both cases, practical music-making demands compression: collapse the infinite into something you can play, something you can transmit.

And in both cases, the art is in choosing what to sacrifice.

## The Harmonic Lattice Is a Codebook

Think about just intonation as an uncompressed signal. Every interval has its exact rational frequency ratio: 3/2 for a fifth, 5/4 for a major third, 7/4 for a harmonic seventh. The harmonic lattice — a multidimensional grid where each axis represents a prime factor (2, 3, 5, 7, ...) — gives every interval a unique address. Nothing is approximated. Nothing is lost.

The problem is identical to the problem of uncompressed audio: it's too big to use. An instrument with infinitely many pitches per octave is as impractical as a 1,536 kbps audio stream on a 1995 modem. You need a **codebook** — a finite set of representatives that approximates the full space within perceptual tolerance.

A tuning system is exactly that codebook.

- **12-tone equal temperament** is a codebook of 12 pitch classes, uniformly spaced. It approximates the 3-limit lattice (fifths and octaves) to within 2 cents and the 5-limit lattice (adding thirds) to within 14 cents. This is the MP3 of tuning: aggressive compression, wide compatibility, graceful degradation for most use cases, audible artifacts if you listen closely.

- **Quarter-comma meantone** is a codebook optimized for a specific region of the lattice: 5-limit thirds are exact, but the coverage is limited to about 8 keys. This is like a high-bitrate codec with a narrow frequency band — perfect fidelity in its target range, catastrophic failure outside it (the wolf fifth is the codec's clipping distortion).

- **53-tone equal temperament** is a high-bitrate codec: 53 pitches per octave approximate 5-limit intervals to within 1.4 cents (sub-threshold for most listeners). Perceptually lossless, but the file size — 53 keys per octave — makes the instrument impractical for most contexts.

- **Adaptive just intonation** (as in some synthesizer implementations) is variable bitrate encoding: the system adjusts the tuning in real time to match the local harmonic context, allocating precision where it's needed. The analogue in audio codecs is VBR MP3 or Opus: quiet passages get fewer bits, complex passages get more.

The mapping isn't just suggestive. It's structurally precise.

## Quantization Error Is the Comma

In a neural audio codec, the continuous latent representation is pushed through a **vector quantizer** — forced onto the nearest point in a discrete codebook. The difference between the original vector and its quantized approximation is the **quantization error**. Minimize this error (in a perceptually weighted sense) and you get a good codec. Let it grow and you get artifacts: metallic ringing, pitch wobble, spectral smearing.

In a tuning system, the continuous space of rational intervals is forced onto the nearest point in a discrete pitch set. The difference between the pure interval and its tempered approximation is **the comma** (or more precisely, the tempering of the comma). Minimize this error and you get a usable temperament. Let it accumulate and you get the wolf — the auditory equivalent of a codec artifact.

The Pythagorean comma (23.46 cents) is the quantization error that accumulates when you try to encode the 3-limit lattice with a 12-element codebook and uniform spacing. The syntonic comma (21.5 cents) is the quantization error between the 3-limit approximation of a major third and its 5-limit pure form. The schisma (1.95 cents) is a second-order quantization artifact — the error in the error.

Well-temperament systems (Werckmeister, Kirnberger, Vallotti) are handcrafted codebooks: irregularly spaced pitch representatives chosen so that the quantization error is distributed according to musical priorities. Commonly used keys get lower distortion; remote keys absorb more. This is **perceptual quantization** — the same strategy as JPEG's frequency-dependent quantization tables, which allocate more precision to spatial frequencies the eye is sensitive to and less to those it isn't.

## Critical Bandwidth Is the Masking Threshold

Why does equal temperament work at all? Its major thirds are 14 cents sharp — objectively, measurably wrong, and audible as beating to anyone who listens for it. The answer is _not_ that the ear can't hear the error. It can. The answer is **cultural tolerance** — a learned acceptance of a specific beating rate as the "normal" sound of a third, reinforced by centuries of keyboard-dominated music.

The critical band mechanism actually works _against_ equal temperament here: because the partials of a tempered third fall within the same critical band but don't perfectly align, they produce audible roughness. A just major third (5:4) is noticeably smoother. Equal temperament succeeds not because the comma is "masked" (it isn't), but because — like a lossy codec whose artifacts you stop noticing after years of MP3 listening — the beating has become part of the expected sound. The codec analogy holds, but the mechanism is habituation, not perceptual invisibility.

This is why 12-TET conquered the world despite its measurable imperfections, exactly as MP3 conquered digital audio despite its measurable artifacts. Both are "good enough" compression for the dominant use case — and both have passionate communities of people who insist the artifacts are unacceptable (audiophiles and just intonation advocates, respectively, sometimes the same people).

## The Codec Learns; The Temperament Was Designed

Here's where the parallel becomes genuinely illuminating rather than merely clever.

Neural audio codecs **learn** their codebooks. Given a bottleneck and a training signal, the vector quantizer converges on a set of representatives that minimizes perceptual loss. Nobody hand-designs the codebook entries. The training process is an empirical discovery of what matters in audio.

Tuning systems, historically, were **hand-designed**. Craftsmen, theorists, and musicians argued for centuries about how to distribute the commas — where to place the quantization error. Each temperament was an assertion about musical priorities: meantone said "thirds matter more than key range"; equal temperament said "all keys must be equally usable"; Werckmeister said "near keys should be sweeter, far keys tolerable."

But something interesting happened in the 20th century. Erv Wilson, Kraig Grady, and the broader microtonality community began developing tuning systems through systematic mathematical exploration — continued fractions, moment-of-symmetry scales, combination product sets. These methods don't start from a desired set of intervals and work backward to a temperament. They explore the **space of possible temperaments** and discover which ones have desirable properties. The approach is closer to neural network training than to classical instrument tuning: set up the constraints, explore the space, let the structure reveal what works.

The regular temperament paradigm formalized by Paul Erlich, Gene Ward Smith, and others makes this explicit. A regular temperament is defined by which commas it **tempers out** — sets to zero. The choice of tempered commas is exactly the choice of what quantization errors the system treats as negligible. Meantone tempers out the syntonic comma (81:80). 12-TET tempers out both the syntonic and Pythagorean commas. Miracle temperament tempers out 225:224 and 1029:1024. Each choice defines a different compression scheme with different fidelity characteristics.

The **Tenney height** of a comma (the log of its numerator times its denominator) functions as a measure of psychoacoustic salience — larger commas are more audible, like louder spectral components in an audio signal. Tempering out a comma of high Tenney height is like throwing away a loud frequency component: it produces a big, audible artifact. Tempering out a comma of low Tenney height is perceptually cheap — like discarding a quiet overtone that was already masked. The optimal temperament, like the optimal codec, eliminates only what won't be missed.

## Rate-Distortion Theory for Tuning

Information theory gives us the **rate-distortion function**: for a given source and distortion measure, there's a theoretical minimum bitrate needed to represent the source within that distortion tolerance. Below this rate, faithful representation is impossible. Above it, you're wasting bits.

There should be an analogous function for tuning: given a harmonic lattice (the source), a perceptual distance measure (the distortion metric), and a number of notes per octave (the rate), what's the minimum achievable error?

This isn't speculative — it's calculable. For the 5-limit lattice with a distortion metric based on cents deviation weighted by interval simplicity, 12 notes per octave achieves a specific error profile. 19 notes does better in the 5-limit but requires more "bandwidth." 31 notes approximates the 7-limit well. 53 is nearly perceptually lossless for the 5-limit. 72 handles the 11-limit. Each step up the note count is like increasing the bitrate: more resolution, diminishing returns, a point beyond which you're spending capacity the ear can't use.

The fascinating implication: there may be an information-theoretic proof that 12-TET is _optimal_ (or near-optimal) for the 5-limit lattice under plausible perceptual weighting. Not merely convenient, not merely traditional — _information-theoretically efficient_. The historical convergence on 12 notes might reflect something deeper than cultural accident.

## Timbral Codecs and Spectral Temperaments

The parallel runs in the other direction too. The Equalizer paper's shape-gain decomposition — separating _what a sound sounds like_ from _how loud it is_ — mirrors the way tuning systems separate _harmonic function_ from _absolute pitch_. In both cases, a factorization that respects perceptual independence leads to better compression.

William Sethares took this further than anyone with his work on tuning to timbre: the observation that consonance depends not on intervals alone but on the interaction between intervals and the spectrum of the instrument playing them. Stretched octaves sound right on a piano because the piano's overtones are themselves stretched (inharmonicity from string stiffness). Gamelan tunings match gamelan spectra. The "correct" tuning is the one whose codebook is matched to the spectral source — exactly as an audio codec performs best when its codebook is matched to the statistics of its training data.

A codec trained on speech develops codebook entries optimized for speech spectra. A codec trained on music develops different entries. A **tuning system matched to a timbre** is a codebook optimized for that timbre's spectral statistics. Sethares made the tuning-codec connection literal: the instrument's spectrum defines the "training data," and the tuning is the codebook that minimizes perceptual distortion for that specific source.

## What the Parallel Reveals

Seeing temperament as compression and codecs as temperament isn't just a tidy analogy. It generates specific insights:

1. **The "good enough" threshold is perceptual, not mathematical.** Just as there's no objectively correct bitrate for audio (it depends on the listener, the content, and the context), there's no objectively correct tuning. The question is always: _what errors can your ear tolerate in this musical situation?_

2. **Compression artifacts are compositional features.** Equal temperament's slightly sharp thirds give major keys a brightness that pure thirds lack. Meantone's wolf gives remote keys a wildness that can be compositionally exploited. MP3's pre-echo artifacts in transients became an aesthetic of early-2000s electronic music. The errors aren't just tolerable — they become part of the sound.

3. **The codec–temperament duality suggests new tuning strategies.** If neural codecs can _learn_ optimal codebooks for audio, could a similar process discover optimal tuning systems for a given musical style? Train a system on a corpus of gamelan music and let it discover the tuning; train it on barbershop quartet recordings and let it find the temperament that minimizes perceptual roughness. The regular temperament paradigm already works this way conceptually — the neural approach would make it empirical.

4. **Both are fundamentally about respecting the ear.** The history of temperament is the history of understanding what the ear can and can't hear. The development of audio codecs is the same history, compressed into decades instead of centuries. They converge because they're solving the same problem: representing a rich, continuous reality through a sparse, discrete code, guided by the only distortion measure that matters — the one between your ears.

---

_The Pythagorean comma is 23.46 cents. A 128 kbps MP3 discards about 90% of the audio data. In both cases, most people can't tell. The ear is the most forgiving critic — and the most demanding one. It forgives enormous quantitative losses as long as the qualitative structure is preserved. The art of temperament, like the art of compression, is knowing where structure lives._
