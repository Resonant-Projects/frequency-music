# Feedback: The Prime Dimensions: Factoring the Harmony of the Primes

## Overall Impression

This essay explores the deep, foundational relationship between prime factorization and musical tuning systems (Euler’s tonnetz, p-limit tuning). It is mathematically sound and presents a classic musicological framework in a clear, modern light. However, the attempt to map high-level prime limits (11, 13) onto functional harmony feels forced and lacks grounding in actual acoustic perception.

## Structure and Argument

The core argument—that prime numbers form the orthogonal axes of musical pitch space—is rigorously argued. The "Lattice of Primes" section provides an excellent visualization of how tuning systems expand geometrically.

However, the "High-Prime Harmony" section falls apart under psychoacoustic scrutiny. You argue that extending the lattice into the 7-limit, 11-limit, and 13-limit opens up new, usable harmonic dimensions. This is mathematically true, but perceptually fraught.

Human pitch perception (pitch class recognition) evolved to track harmonic spectra produced by physical objects (strings, tubes, vocal cords). The harmonic series decreases in amplitude as it goes up. By the time you reach the 11th and 13th harmonics, their physical amplitude is usually so low that they are masked by the lower partials. If you artificially boost an 11-limit ratio (like 11:8) and play it as a chord, it doesn't sound like a "new dimension of harmony"; it sounds like an out-of-tune fourth, because the ear's harmonic template is overwhelmingly weighted toward the primes 2, 3, and 5. Claiming that the 11-limit is a "dimension of functional harmony" ignores the biological limits of the cochlea's pattern-matching algorithms. It is a mathematical extrapolation that ignores the acoustic substrate.

The "Dimensional Collapse" section is excellent. Your explanation of equal temperament not as a compromise, but as a deliberate mathematical "folding" of infinite prime dimensions into a finite loop, is one of the best defenses of 12-TET in this entire collection.

## Clarity and Flow

The mathematical exposition is clean. Walking the reader from 2 (octave) to 3 (fifth) to 5 (major third) provides a clear, step-by-step introduction to rational intonation.

## Style and Voice

The tone is academic, authoritative, and slightly speculative.

"Equal temperament is a dimensionality reduction algorithm. It projects an infinite-dimensional prime lattice onto a 1D circle." This is a fantastic, cross-disciplinary aphorism. It bridges math, music, and data science perfectly.

## Line-Level Edits

> "The 7-limit introduces the harmonic seventh (7:4), a significantly flatter, more 'bluesy' interval than the minor seventh of 12-TET."
> **Critique:** This is a very common, but highly debated, ethnomusicological claim. Many scholars argue the "blue note" is not a static 7:4 ratio, but a fluid, continuous pitch region that defies strict rational classification. Tying the blues strictly to the 7th harmonic over-intellectualizes a culturally derived, expressive intonation practice. Consider softening this to: "often compared to certain 'bluesy' intonations."

> "What if a piece modulated not by moving along the circle of fifths (the 3-axis), but by shifting the entire harmonic structure up the 7-axis or 11-axis?"
> **Critique:** This is a great theoretical question, but practically, Ben Johnston and Harry Partch already did this. You should name-drop them here to prove that this isn't just a hypothetical thought experiment; it's a real, historical branch of avant-garde composition.
