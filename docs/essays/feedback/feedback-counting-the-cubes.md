# Feedback — "Counting the Cubes: Burnside's Lemma and the Enumeration of Musical Objects"

## Overall Impression
This is one of the stronger pieces in the series. It has a clear premise, an actual mathematical result to explain, a strong through-line from LeWitt to Forte to Toussaint, and it earns its conclusion. The subhead framing does real work. What holds the essay back from being excellent is a tendency to gesture at richness ("perceptual parsimony," "atlas of possibility") in place of harder claims about what any of this means compositionally.

## Structure / Argument
- The LeWitt → pitch classes → rhythms → scales → navigation arc is well-built. Each section adds a new instance of Burnside without repeating the previous one.
- The "From Enumeration to Navigation" section is where the essay softens. It invokes Tonnetz, OPTIC spaces, and rhythmic necklace lattices in one paragraph without doing any of them the courtesy of a sentence of explanation. Readers who don't already know these frameworks get name-checked, not taught.
- The "Deeper Pattern" closing paragraph makes a claim ("the symmetry groups… are the symmetries of the perceptual spaces we inhabit") that is philosophically loaded and evidentially thin. Rotational invariance of rhythmic necklaces is not obviously a perceptual property of rhythm — many listeners hear a downbeat-shifted pattern as a different pattern. The essay would be sharper if it acknowledged this rather than gliding past.
- The complement-theorem aside ("every hexachord's complement belongs to the same or a Z-related set class") is dropped in as a "direct consequence of this arithmetic" — but it isn't, really. It's a separate result. The essay should either prove the connection or delete the claim.

## Clarity / Flow
- The table is a real asset. More essays in this series should use structured presentation like this.
- "though mathematical abundance doesn't automatically mean aesthetic richness" — good self-check; keep the habit.
- The transition "The connection to LeWitt goes deeper" is stronger than what follows it. The connection announced is that both apply Burnside to constrained subsets, which is not deeper than what's already been established — it's the same connection restated.

## Style / Voice
- Consistent, readable, and appropriately technical. This voice works.
- "The mathematical hero of the story" is a cute gesture, but "hero" gets deployed lightly; consider the opening's tone against the closing's earnestness.
- "the same moment: the transition from 'I can list them all' to 'I understand why there are this many'" — genuinely nice.

## Line-Level Notes
- "the number of distinct objects equals the average number of objects fixed by each symmetry operation" — worth adding one sentence of intuition. This is the whole engine of the essay.
- Numbers should be verified: is the 224 set-classes figure (with Z-related pairs treated as distinct or merged?) the commonly cited one? Same with 4,116 necklaces for n=16. These are precise claims and should be sourced or footnoted.
- "there is no undiscovered 7-note scale in 12-TET" — technically true in a well-defined sense, but the phrasing invites the reader to overread it. Add "up to transposition" or similar.
- The closing coda paragraph is trying to be lyrical and lands as slightly sentimental. Cut or tighten.
