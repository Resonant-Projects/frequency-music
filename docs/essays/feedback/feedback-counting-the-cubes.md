# Feedback: Counting the Cubes: Burnside's Lemma and the Enumeration of Musical Objects (Second Review)

**Overall Impression:**
The revisions to this essay are excellent. You've addressed the core critique from the first review—that the math felt disconnected from compositional reality—by refining the tone and clarifying the relationship between mathematical abundance and musical utility. The essay now feels much more like a genuine exploration of musical structure rather than just a mathematical summary.

**Structure and Argument:**
The structural flow remains strong (Cubes -> Chords -> Rhythms -> Scales). The key improvements are in how you frame the implications of the math:

*   **Hexachords:** You specifically addressed the critique about equating combinatorial abundance with aesthetic richness. The revised text ("which is one reason twelve-tone composers found them so fertile (though mathematical abundance doesn't automatically mean aesthetic richness)") is a crucial and much-appreciated nuance.
*   **The Sculptor's Problem & Rhythm:** You beautifully clarified the connection between LeWitt's connectivity constraints and rhythmic parseability. Acknowledging that the physical constraints (topology vs. biological entrainment) are different, even while the combinatorial math is the same, strengthens the argument immensely. It shows you aren't just forcing a metaphor to fit.
*   **Navigation:** The conclusion feels much more earned now. By framing Burnside's lemma as the first step in building an "atlas" (like LeWitt's installation), you give the enumeration a clear purpose: understanding the shape and size of the possibility space before trying to navigate it (topology).

**Clarity and Flow:**
The text is clean, precise, and easy to follow. The transition from the abstract math of pitch class sets to the visceral reality of rhythms and scales works very well. 

**Style and Voice:**
The voice is confident and analytical. The analogy of the "atlas" in the "From Enumeration to Navigation" section is a fantastic addition that grounds the abstract math in a visual, spatial metaphor.

**Final Thoughts:**
This is a significantly stronger piece than the first draft. The math now serves the music, rather than the other way around. It successfully demonstrates *why* understanding the size and shape of the possibility space matters, even if it doesn't tell you exactly which notes to play. Great work.
