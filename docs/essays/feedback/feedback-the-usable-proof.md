# Feedback: The Usable Proof

## Overall Impression

The essay offers a productive distinction between a structure’s stipulated existence and the evidence that makes it actionable for a listener or model. Its practical vocabulary—proof length, redundancy, locality, fragility—is the strongest contribution. Yet “proof” does too much work. Formal proof, statistical evidence, perceptual cueing, dataset certification, and contextual recognition obey different standards. The disclaimer “not a formal proof” helps, but it does not prevent later claims such as “effective unprovability” from borrowing mathematical force without preserving mathematical meaning.

Factual framing needs tightening. “A vulnerability can exist but be impossible to prove” is ambiguous: impossible under which proof system, computational bound, information regime, or empirical protocol? The zero-knowledge source must be cited directly and summarized precisely enough that the analogy rests on its actual result rather than a dramatic paraphrase.

## Structure and Argument

The essay’s sequence is clear: principle, source evidence, composition, conclusion. The opening and “Sourcehood Needs Evidence” substantially duplicate one another, however. Both cycle through SR-CorrNet, FSD50K-Solo, and anomaly detection in nearly the same order. Condense the opening to establish the analogy, then let the second section scrutinize where it holds and where it breaks.

The argument would benefit from an explicit middle premise: a structure can affect perception before a listener can consciously identify it. That complicates the binary “true/usable” schema in an interesting way. “Usable” should be task-relative: usable for stream segregation, prediction, recognition, emotional response, or verbal report? Without a specified act, evidence cannot be declared sufficient.

The ending is attractive but too universal. “An experience becoming reliable enough to use” begs the question of reliable for whom and for what. End by returning to the four compositional controls and proposing them as hypotheses to test, not as settled perceptual variables.

## Clarity and Flow

The phrase “true and usable” is lucid as a compositional instruction but philosophically unstable. A hidden tonic is not “true” in the same sense as a speaker’s physical presence or a mathematical proposition. Prefer “specified in the compositional system” versus “perceptually available.” This keeps the essay from treating formal, physical, and interpretive facts as equivalent.

“A rhythm becomes meter” and “a room becomes space” are rhetorically smooth but technically vague. Meter may be inferred probabilistically from rhythm and context; a room does not become space, but reflections support estimates of enclosure, distance, or direction. Define the intended perceptual operations.

## Style and Voice

The prose is controlled and memorable, especially “make its proof too expensive.” Preserve that metaphor while marking its scope. The repeated triad “a spatial cue, a stable F0 contour, a correlation pattern…” gives texture, but similar lists recur enough to feel formulaic. One carefully explained example would add more authority than another inventory.

Avoid using “proof,” “certify,” “undeniable,” and “truth” in quick succession unless their standards are specified. These words make the essay sound more rigorous while sometimes reducing precision.

## Line-Level Edits

- “A vulnerability can exist but be impossible to prove” → “A vulnerability may be real yet lack a feasible proof under a specified computational or evidentiary constraint.”
- “A structure only matters to a listening system when there is a usable proof of it.” → “A structure can guide a listening system only insofar as the system has task-relevant evidence for it.” This allows subliminal or indirect effects without demanding formal recognition.
- “The label ‘single source’ is not a usable training condition.” → “The label is a noisy training condition unless the curation procedure supplies credible evidence that competing sources are absent or negligible.”
- “Nothing ontological changed in the score.” → “The compositional rule stayed fixed; the cues available to the listener changed.” This avoids an unnecessary ontological claim.
- “A rhythm becomes meter when its evidence is strong enough.” → “A listener infers a metrical organization when rhythmic and contextual cues support one strongly enough for the listening task.”
- “proof in music is not a theorem” → “musical ‘proof’ here means perceptual evidence becoming reliable enough to guide expectation.” This closes by defining rather than romanticizing the metaphor.
