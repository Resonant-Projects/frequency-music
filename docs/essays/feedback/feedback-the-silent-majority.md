# Feedback: The Silent Majority: Why Unheard Frequencies Drive the Music

## Overall Impression

This essay attempts to prove that ultrasonic and sub-sonic frequencies profoundly impact musical perception, despite being outside the range of conscious hearing. While the premise is intriguing and supported by some audiophile lore, the essay stretches the physics of intermodulation distortion to justify a somewhat mystical view of "inaudible sound."

## Structure and Argument

The core argument rests on two physical phenomena: Intermodulation Distortion (IMD) in the high end, and tactile perception (bone conduction) in the low end.

The "Tactile Bass" section is biologically and acoustically sound. The fact that the body feels frequencies below 20Hz (in the chest cavity and skeleton) even if the cochlea doesn't register them as "pitch" is a well-documented physiological fact. This perfectly explains the physical impact of a club sound system.

However, the "Ultrasonic Beating" section overstates the case for high-resolution audio (like 96kHz sample rates). You argue that frequencies above 20kHz interact in the air (or in the ear) to create difference tones (IMD) that fold down into the audible range. While technically true, the _amplitude_ of these difference tones is generally microscopic—often masked entirely by the noise floor or the actual audible content of the music. Treating these barely-measurable ultrasonic ghosts as the "secret to analog warmth" is a massive leap that most acousticians would reject. You are taking an extreme edge-case of nonlinear distortion and treating it as a primary driver of aesthetic quality.

The "Oohashi Effect" section (referencing the controversial study on the "hypersonic effect") requires much more skepticism. You present Oohashi's findings—that ultrasonic frequencies trigger increased EEG alpha rhythms—as established fact. This study has been notoriously difficult to replicate and is heavily criticized for methodological flaws. Building a grand theory of musical perception on a disputed 25-year-old paper weakens the essay's credibility.

## Clarity and Flow

The explanation of intermodulation distortion is clear enough for a layperson to understand how two high frequencies can create a lower one ($f_2 - f_1$).

## Style and Voice

The tone occasionally slips into audiophile mysticism ("The music we hear is just the shadow of the music that exists").

## Line-Level Edits

> "When an analog synthesizer generates a saw wave, it contains harmonics extending well past 40kHz. When a digital synthesizer generates the same wave at a 44.1kHz sample rate, those upper harmonics are violently brick-wall filtered."
> **Critique:** "Violently" is an emotional word applied to a mathematical filter. More importantly, those harmonics _must_ be filtered (anti-aliasing) to prevent them from folding back down into the audible range as dissonant, atonal garbage (aliasing). You are framing the Nyquist limit as an aesthetic tragedy, rather than the mathematical necessity that makes digital audio possible at all.

> "Even if we cannot 'hear' 30kHz, the interaction of 30kHz and 32kHz creates a 2kHz difference tone right in the most sensitive range of human hearing."
> **Critique:** This implies the 2kHz difference tone is loud and important. It is usually vanishingly quiet. You need to add a caveat about amplitude, or this reads like pseudoscience.
