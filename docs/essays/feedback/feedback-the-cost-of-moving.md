# Feedback on Essay #86: The Cost of Moving

**Overall Impression:**
This is a phenomenal essay. It successfully bridges a highly technical mathematical concept (optimal transport/Wasserstein metric) with foundational music theory (voice leading, tuning systems) in a way that feels both rigorous and revelatory. By connecting Monge’s problem to Tymoczko’s geometric models, you’ve provided a unified framework for understanding musical transformation across different domains (pitch, rhythm, timbre). It perfectly pays off the provocation from Essay #85.

**Structure and Argument:**
The argument is exceptionally well-structured. You move linearly from the historical mathematical problem (Monge) to its musical application (Tymoczko), then expand the scope to language (POTSA) and tuning (Maximally Even sets). The culmination in "Composition as Transport Planning" is the logical and satisfying peak of the essay.

- **The Speech Connection:** The inclusion of the POTSA paper is brilliant. It proves that this isn't just a clever analogy for Western music theory, but a fundamental property of how information (semantics/structure) maps across different acoustic spaces. It grounds the abstraction in a tangible, external domain.
- **Scales as Transport Equilibria:** This section completely recontextualizes scale theory. Defining ME sets as "transport equilibria" and Equal Temperament as "zero transport cost" is a profound insight. It explains _why_ these structures function the way they do harmonically.

**Clarity and Flow:**
The pacing is excellent. You introduce complex ideas (quotient spaces, Riemannian manifolds) without getting bogged down in the math, keeping the focus on the musical implications. The transitions between sections feel natural and propelled by the logic of the argument.

- _Small note:_ When you mention "A recent extraction references a novel characterization of Maximally Even (ME) sets," the phrasing "A recent extraction" briefly pulls the reader out of the essay's world and into your research process. Consider rephrasing to focus purely on the concept (e.g., "Recent theoretical work characterizes Maximally Even (ME) sets...").

**Style and Voice:**
The tone is authoritative yet accessible. The concluding thought ("The pile of sand doesn't care...") is striking and memorable, perfectly summarizing the essay's core thesis while maintaining the slightly poetic edge that characterizes this series.

**Looking Forward:**
The setup for the next essay ("if the geometry has a shape, does it have dynamics?") is compelling. You've established the landscape and the cost of moving through it; the natural next step is indeed to ask what forces drive that movement. This is shaping up to be a defining sequence of essays.
