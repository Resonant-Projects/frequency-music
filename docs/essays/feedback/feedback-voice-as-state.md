# Feedback: Voice As State

## Overall Impression

The essay’s central proposal—that composers can write changes in a voice’s perceptual condition rather than only its nominal part—is strong. The axes of identity, agency, stability, locality, latency, and context-dependence create a useful studio vocabulary. The greatest weakness is the clinical passage: ALS is used as an evocative illustration of “deformation,” then the exercise invites states labeled “healthy” and “damaged.” This risks aestheticizing disability and implying that acoustic instability maps cleanly onto disease, intention, or agency.

The essay should distinguish clinical measurement from listener interpretation. Speech biomarkers are statistical predictors evaluated against clinical labels; listeners’ impressions of exhaustion, illness, intimacy, or confidence are culturally mediated and often unreliable. A cue can influence response without making the inferred condition factually “real.”

## Structure and Argument

The opening lists six studies and asserts one premise, but the commonality is loose. Source separation estimates streams, streaming translation decides when to emit, ASR metrics exploit linguistic context, binaural rendering tracks location, and SAND predicts disease progression. All concern time-varying evidence, yet only some model an explicit “state.” Define state as the essay’s compositional abstraction rather than attributing it to every source.

“The Moving Target” and “Evidence Before Naming” both establish that identity depends on multiple cues. Use the first to define the state variables and the second to distinguish task-specific evidence: sufficient for transcription is not sufficient for naturalness, identity, localization, or clinical inference.

The studio exercise is productive but needs ethical and methodological revision. Replace “healthy, damaged” with acoustic descriptions such as stable/unstable phonation, intact/disrupted timing, or high/low intelligibility. Explicitly say the transformations should not be presented as simulations of ALS. The final contextual transformation is also not “preserve only context”; rhythm, harmony, and spatial trace are direct evidence derived from the phrase. Call it contextual reinstatement or learned expectation.

The ending returns cleanly to the title but remains abstract. A stronger conclusion would state that every “state” is relative to a listening task and should be described with observable acoustic or perceptual variables.

## Clarity and Flow

“Voice identity” and “voice state” remain underdefined. Identity can mean speaker recognition, stream continuity, or musical-role continuity. State includes physical production, acoustic realization, inferred affect, spatial position, and listener certainty. These levels should not sit on a single axis without labels.

“Agency” is particularly unstable. Stretching attacks or flattening dynamics may reduce perceived assertiveness, but it does not establish that a voice has less agency, nor should atypical motor speech be equated with reduced intention. Rename it “perceived initiative/completion” and make clear that it is a listener judgment.

Technical assertions require direct citations. Report what the SAND challenge predicts, its labels and evaluation design, and avoid implying validated disease-progression inference unless longitudinal evidence supports that exact claim.

## Style and Voice

The essay is direct and musically suggestive. “It answers the transcription question while missing the state question” is an effective contrast. Yet “the mechanisms that let speech stay speech” and “trajectory of the voice’s ability” dramatize disability in a way that is both imprecise and ethically charged. Prefer clinical specificity and person-centered language.

## Line-Level Edits

- “a voice is not just a source label. It is a state evolving through evidence.” → “For these tasks, a vocal signal is interpreted through time-varying evidence about source, content, location, and condition.”
- “changing acoustic patterns can reveal disease progression” → “speech features may help predict clinically assessed ALS status or progression; specify which outcome the challenge evaluates.”
- “Progressive dysarthria is … a gradual deformation” → “Progressive dysarthria can involve changes in timing, phonation, articulation, and intelligibility.” Avoid framing a person’s speech as deformed.
- “The cue is real because it changes what the listener can predict” → “The cue has a perceptual effect if it changes prediction or response; that does not validate the listener’s interpretation of illness or emotion.”
- “identity: whether the source remains followable as itself” → “stream identity: whether listeners bind successive events to the same perceived source.”
- “weaken agency” → “reduce perceived initiative or phrase completion,” followed by a caution that this is an aesthetic judgment, not a claim about the speaker.
- “present, healthy, damaged” → “present, intelligible, stable, masked, distant, or remembered.”
- “write the state of the voice” → “write observable and perceived changes in the vocal event, while keeping bodily or clinical interpretations explicitly provisional.”
