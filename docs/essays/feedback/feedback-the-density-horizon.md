# Feedback: The Density Horizon: Where Notes Dissolve Into Texture

## Overall Impression
This essay is spectacular. It combines empirical data (Amanous Disklavier tests) with historical theory (Stockhausen, Xenakis) and fundamental physics (Gabor limit) to create a highly actionable, rigorously proven musicological concept. The synthesis is tight, the analogies are accurate, and the compositional takeaways are genuinely useful.

## Structure and Argument
The core argument—that a specific mathematical threshold exists where human perception switches from tracking individual events to tracking statistical textures—is beautifully argued. 

The "Continuum's Tollgate" section flawlessly bridges Stockhausen's theoretical continuum with hard data (24-30 notes/second). Pointing out that this threshold aligns exactly with the lower limit of human pitch perception (~20 Hz) is a "drop the mic" moment of scientific synthesis. It proves that the Amanous algorithm didn't just find a glitch in its own metrics; it mapped the actual biological limits of the human cochlea. 

The "Restoration" section is the only place where the essay slightly loses focus. The transition from the forward process (Amanous generating texture) to the backward process (MSR recovering stems) is conceptually interesting but physically mismatched. In Amanous, the "loss of information" is a biological, perceptual illusion (the notes are still discrete in the MIDI file/piano roll; the *ear* blurs them). In Music Source Restoration, the loss of information is a physical, mathematical reality (the waveforms are summed into a single track). Comparing a perceptual illusion to a physical summation slightly muddles the otherwise ironclad physics of the essay.

## Clarity and Flow
The "Phase Map of Musical Time" section is one of the best summaries in the entire repository. Breaking down the Hz ranges from Form (<1Hz) to Rhythm to Horizon to Pitch is an incredibly clarifying pedagogical tool. 

## Style and Voice
The tone is authoritative, analytical, and respectful of both the composers and the scientists it cites. 

"The question presupposes a level of resolution that the signal doesn't support... Asking 'how many notes are playing right now?' at 30 notes per second is like asking 'what color is this pixel?' when looking at an impressionist painting from across the room." This is world-class explanatory writing.

**Critique:** This is a perfect, concise summary of the Gabor limit applied to cognition. No changes needed.
