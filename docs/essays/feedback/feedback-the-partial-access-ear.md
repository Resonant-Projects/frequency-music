# Feedback: The Partial-Access Ear
## Overall Impression
The essay has a concise and resonant premise: access to musical structure is distributed across time, context, consciousness, and computational resources. It is also the least evidentially secure of the group. The anesthesia source is explicitly “only partially extracted,” yet it supports the most consequential claim; proof complexity is generalized without a precise theorem or resource model; and claims about bodily versus conscious listening are presented as fact without operational definitions.

The essay should not publish the anesthesia paragraph in its current form. A partial extraction is insufficient for a medically sensitive empirical claim. Read and cite the underlying study, specify anesthesia type and depth, distinguish neural response, implicit memory, conditioning, and learning, and report sample size and controls. If that verification is unavailable, remove the claim rather than hedging it poetically.

## Structure and Argument
The four cases map partiality onto temporal, contextual, conscious, and computational access. That parallel structure is elegant, but it hides large category differences. Speech translation and anomaly detection concern missing inputs or metadata; anesthesia concerns states of consciousness and memory; proof complexity concerns formal derivability under resource bounds. Define “access” abstractly—information available to an agent for a specified action—then state that these are analogies across domains, not one empirical mechanism.

The proof-complexity paragraph says truths “may be provable in principle but inaccessible in practice because the proof is too long to ever write down.” This could refer to proof length lower bounds, feasible provability, or cryptographic assumptions; each is different. “Too long to ever write down” is rhetoric, not a technical criterion. Name the formal system, asymptotic claim, and assumptions, then limit the musical translation to resource-bounded recognition.

The practical-handles list is useful, but “let unconscious or bodily cues carry structure” rests on an unresolved distinction. Bodily response can be conscious; unconscious processing is not directly equivalent to anesthesia learning. Recast this as designing cues that support sensorimotor entrainment before explicit verbal identification, a more testable claim.

The ending asks the right question and could be made more defensible by adding “for which task.” Access is never simply possessed; it is access sufficient to detect, predict, move, remember, or describe.

## Clarity and Flow
“The listener almost never receives the whole system” is metaphorically appealing but unclear. A listener receives acoustic energy shaped by a performance and environment, not “the system.” Specify whether the unavailable whole is causal structure, score, source labels, future context, or compositional design.

“Harmonic direction might be formally present but require a proof too long for real-time listening” conflates mathematical proof with perceptual inference. A complex analysis may be unavailable during playback without the music’s direction being formally encoded or deductively provable. Use “require more evidence or computation than real-time listening permits.”

“Obvious to memory” is also puzzling: memory can make a pattern obvious on later hearings, but a property is not available to memory in isolation. Name repeated listening or retrospective recognition.

## Style and Voice
The compactness helps the essay; its images—pulse reaching the body, a room revealing geometry—invite compositional application. Yet the lyrical register repeatedly lends certainty to speculative claims. Retain the voice while tagging epistemic status: “suggests,” “could model,” and “by analogy” are necessary here.

The title is evocative but “ear” narrows a piece concerned with models, formal systems, bodies, and consciousness. That productive tension should be acknowledged, perhaps by making the ear the figure for any resource-limited listener rather than silently shifting agents.

## Line-Level Edits
- “The listener almost never receives the whole system” → “A listener rarely has access to every cause, label, future event, and structural relation shaping what is heard.”
- “Its knowledge is deliberately unfinished” → “Its input context is necessarily incomplete at emission time.” Models do not possess “knowledge” in the same sense as listeners.
- “performance depends on whether the model can infer the hidden source” → quantify the dependency and clarify whether identity is machine type, instance, or operating condition.
- “auditory learning can persist below conscious awareness” → replace with the study’s exact measured outcome after reviewing the full paper; do not infer learning from neural discrimination alone.
- “the sound reaches the body” → “auditory stimulation may elicit measurable processing”; this avoids an unsupported mind/body binary.
- “Some truths may be provable…” → name the proof-complexity result and resource bound.
- “Metric pulse might reach the body before the conscious ear names it” → “A listener may entrain to pulse before being able to report or label the meter.” Add supporting evidence.
- “obvious to memory and unavailable to first hearing” → “recognizable on repeated hearing but difficult to infer on the first.”
- “who gets access…” → “which agent gets access to which evidence, when, and for what action?”
