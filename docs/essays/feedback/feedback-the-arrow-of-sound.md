# Feedback: The Arrow of Sound: Why Music Only Goes Forward

## Overall Impression
This essay is conceptually strong, tackling the fundamental temporal asymmetry of music. The integration of thermodynamics, information theory, and psychoacoustics is generally well-executed. However, the essay occasionally slips into hyperbole, presenting standard musical facts as profound philosophical mysteries, and stretches the "entropy" metaphor a bit too far. 

## Structure and Argument
The progression of the argument is logical: Phenomenological observation (retrograde sounds bad) -> Cognitive mechanism (prediction engine) -> Physical mechanism (entropy/envelopes) -> Cultural mechanism (sonata form) -> Mathematical mechanism (information theory). 

However, the "Entropy and the Score" section conflates two different definitions of entropy. Acoustic entropy (the decay of a piano string into heat/silence) is thermodynamic. Harmonic "entropy" (the progression from tonic to dominant and back) is purely informational/cultural. Conflating the two under the single banner of the "second law" is a category error. A V7 chord does not have higher thermodynamic entropy than an I chord. Using physics vocabulary as a metaphor for music theory is fine, but you state "This is the second law at the scale of single notes," crossing the line from metaphor to literal, incorrect claim.

The sonata form analogy is excellent, but the claim "Reverse a sonata and the consequence precedes the cause" is slightly flawed. If you literally reverse the audio of a sonata, yes, it's gibberish. If you play the *sections* in reverse order (Recapitulation -> Development -> Exposition), you just have a piece that starts complex and gets simpler (a very common trope in modern ambient/electronic music). The classical teleology is violated, but the result isn't "empty"; it's just a different aesthetic goal (decay rather than growth). 

## Clarity and Flow
The explanation of forward vs. backward temporal masking in the "Codec Knows" section is brilliant. It perfectly bridges the gap between human auditory processing and machine engineering, providing hard, empirical evidence for the essay's philosophical claims.

The "Information Theory's Verdict" section is clearly written, but stating "The emotional arc of the piece is the arc of this distribution's entropy: low -> high -> low" is an oversimplification of emotion. Emotional arc maps to *tension*, which correlates with information entropy in classical tonality, but high entropy (total randomness/white noise) is not emotionally "high"; it's usually just boring. 

## Style and Voice
The tone is confident and analytical, but sometimes too definitive. 

"The composer's real instrument isn't the orchestra — it's the listener's probability model." This is a fantastic, highly quotable aphorism. It synthesizes the entire essay into a single, actionable thought. 

## Line-Level Edits

> "Play a Bach fugue subject backwards and it doesn't sound like 'the same thing reversed' — it sounds like a *different, worse melody*."
**Critique:** "Worse" is subjective. It sounds like a different melody because it *is* a different melody. The harmonic implications of the intervals have changed. Just state that it loses its identity and harmonic function; you don't need to pass an aesthetic judgment to prove the point.

> "Stravinsky understood this... The piece's violence comes not from the dissonance *per se* but from the sustained denial of predicted resolution."
**Critique:** This is a very common musicological trope about the *Rite of Spring*, but it's partially false. The dissonance *itself* (the biting polytonal chords) is absolutely a source of the piece's violence. The rhythmic irregularity is another. The "denial of resolution" is a factor, but claiming it is the *sole* source of violence over-intellectualizes a piece that is famous for its raw, visceral, acoustic impact. Don't erase the physical reality of the sound to make your cognitive theory look cleaner.
