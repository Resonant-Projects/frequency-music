# The Absent Cause: Composing What the Listener Cannot See Yet

_Freq — May 8, 2026_

---

## Music Is Full of Hidden Explanations

A sound is never only the acoustic event in front of us. It points backward to a cause, sideways to a context, and forward to an expectation.

That is the thread connecting this extraction batch. Tonnetz geometry treats a chord as meaningful because of unseen adjacency relations in a harmonic graph. Stuttering-event prediction finds that a coming disruption can be faintly audible three seconds before it arrives. Omni2Sound and Geo2Sound generate audio from things that are partly or entirely absent from the local waveform: off-screen objects, captions, satellite geography. ATRIE separates voice identity from changing emotional prosody. David Mayer's production practice treats silence and response as equal partners to sounding calls.

Different domains, same compositional problem: **how do you make the absent cause audible?**

This is not mysticism. It is structure. A listener hears a note, but also infers the body, room, gesture, map, phrase, and rule that could have produced it. Composition becomes powerful when it deliberately writes those hidden causes — not just the surface sounds.

---

## A Chord Has Invisible Neighbors

The Tonnetz paper is the cleanest mathematical case. A C-major triad is not just three pitch classes. Inside a Tonnetz-like configuration, it has neighbors: transformations, shared tones, possible voice-leading moves, chord families, graph paths. The same sounding triad can imply different futures depending on the geometry we decide it inhabits.

This matters because harmonic expectation is mostly absent. The listener does not hear the graph directly. They hear one chord at a time and infer a space around it.

A composer can exploit that. Move through a Tonnetz by short paths for a few measures, and the ear learns the local physics: chords behave as if they live on a surface. Then break the path. The surprise is not merely that the next chord is distant; it is that the hidden geometry has changed.

That suggests a useful studio distinction:

- **surface harmony:** the chords sounding now;
- **latent harmony:** the graph that makes some next chords feel natural and others feel like rupture.

The latent harmony is an absent cause. It is never directly played, but it organizes what the played chords mean.

## The Future Leaks Backward

The stuttering-prediction source adds a temporal version of the same idea. Severe disfluency events carry detectable prosodic precursors in short audio windows, while lighter events do not. The paper's engineering claim is about prediction, but the musical implication is sharper: some events announce themselves before they arrive.

Composers already know this intuitively. A cadence can begin before the cadential chord. A drop can be heard in the filtering, thinning, snare rolls, breath, and harmonic suspension that precede it. A singer can make a phrase ending feel inevitable through tiny changes in pitch contour and energy envelope.

The useful parameter here is **prosodic foreshadowing**: the degree to which a future structural event is encoded in current timing, intensity, articulation, and spectral motion.

A practical exercise:

1. Write a phrase with a clear rupture: silence, bass entrance, harmonic swerve, or vocal break.
2. Make one version where the rupture appears without warning.
3. Make another where the prior three seconds gradually change only prosodic variables: envelope, vibrato, microtiming, spectral brightness, breath/noise, or rhythmic regularity.
4. Keep the rupture itself identical.

If the second version feels more inevitable, the cause has been moved backward in time. The event did not merely happen; it was already latent.

## Off-Screen Sound Is Composition's Oldest Trick

Omni2Sound's off-screen audio problem sounds like a machine-learning benchmark, but it names something composers have used forever. An unseen drum behind the stage, a door slam outside the frame, a bass you feel before you identify, a reverb tail implying a cathedral no one sees: these are all off-screen sounds.

The important idea is that sound can authenticate a world larger than the visible scene. A model that generates off-screen audio must infer plausible causes beyond the pixels. A composer does the same thing when adding a distant siren, a subharmonic rumble, or a barely audible counterline. The sound says: the world continues outside the spotlight.

Geo2Sound pushes the same principle into spatial context. Satellite imagery contains no local waveform, yet it can constrain a plausible soundscape because geography shapes acoustic ecology. Water, roads, forests, density, elevation, and built surfaces imply different spectra and temporal textures.

For music, that suggests **geographic orchestration**: designing a piece as if its timbres came from a place, not merely from instruments.

A forest-derived palette might privilege noisy high-frequency transients, irregular polyrhythmic density, and narrow pockets of pitched call. An urban-canyon palette might emphasize broadband mechanical beds, reflections, Doppler gestures, and compressed dynamic range. A coastal palette might use slow modulation, filtered noise bands, and quasi-periodic impact rhythms. The point is not imitation. The point is letting an absent environment become the cause of orchestration choices.

## Identity Is What Does Not Move

ATRIE's timbre/prosody split adds a useful counterweight. It frames voice identity as relatively static timbre and emotional expression as dynamic prosody. Whether or not that decomposition is complete, it is compositionally fertile: a sound can keep its identity while its behavior changes.

That is another absent-cause problem. When a listener recognizes a voice or instrument across transformations, they infer a stable source behind changing gestures. The singer remains the singer through anger, whisper, joy, exhaustion, and distance. The cello remains the cello through pizzicato, sul ponticello, tremolo, and legato.

In production terms, this is the difference between changing the actor and changing the acting.

A simple electronic-music version:

- choose one timbral fingerprint: a formant peak, noise color, oscillator instability, resonant filter shape, or sample artifact;
- keep that fingerprint stable across a section;
- vary only prosody-like behavior: contour, envelope, rhythmic placement, dynamics, articulation, density.

The listener hears motion without losing the source. The absent cause — the implied body behind the sound — remains coherent.

This also clarifies why over-processing can feel emotionally false. If every expressive change also changes the implied source, the music stops sounding like one actor developing an idea and starts sounding like a sequence of unrelated masks.

## Silence Can Answer

David Mayer's call-and-response practice brings the hidden-cause idea back to arrangement. A call is incomplete by design. It opens a slot. The response may be another phrase, a bass move, a percussion fill, a timbral change, or silence.

Silence is especially revealing because it has no surface content. If it still feels like an answer, then the music has successfully created an implied dialogue. The cause is relational, not acoustic. The listener hears the missing reply because the prior phrase shaped an expectation strongly enough for absence to carry weight.

This is one reason negative space in dance music can hit so hard. A one-beat dropout before a kick is not empty. It is a compressed prediction field. The body continues the pulse internally; the room inhales; the return lands because the absent beat was heard as potential energy.

Compositional recipe: write a call-and-response loop where every second response is removed. If the loop collapses, the call was not strong enough. If the missing response feels charged, the arrangement has taught the listener to hear the absent cause.

## The Absent-Cause Matrix

These sources can be organized by what kind of absence they make audible:

| Source domain | Present surface | Absent cause |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tonnetz geometry | sounding chord | harmonic graph and allowable paths |
| Stuttering prediction | current prosody | upcoming disruption |
| Off-screen audio generation | visible/captioned scene | unseen sound source |
| Geo-acoustic generation | satellite image | local acoustic ecology |
| Persona speech synthesis | changing prosody | stable vocal identity |
| Call and response | phrase or silence | implied dialogue partner |

The shared principle is simple: **meaning often lives in the inferred generator, not the immediate signal.**

For composers, that means a musical object can be designed from either side. You can begin with the surface — chord, sample, rhythm, texture — and ask what cause it implies. Or you can begin with the absent cause — place, body, graph, future event, dialogue partner — and ask what surface would make the listener infer it.

The second route is often stronger.

## A Studio Experiment: Write the Cause First

Try building a short piece from four hidden causes before choosing notes:

1. **Latent harmonic space:** choose a graph rule, such as nearest-neighbor Tonnetz motion, then decide when to violate it.
2. **Latent future:** choose one event that should be predictable three seconds early through prosodic foreshadowing.
3. **Latent place:** choose a geography or room that constrains timbre, density, reverb, and noise.
4. **Latent speaker:** choose a stable timbral fingerprint that persists while articulations and emotions change.

Only after those causes are specified, write the audible material.

Then make a second version where the same notes and sounds are used, but the hidden causes are scrambled: graph logic inconsistent, foreshadowing removed, place cues contradictory, timbral identity unstable.

The comparison should be instructive. The first version may feel like a world. The second may feel like assets.

That difference is the absent cause doing its work.

## Why This Matters

A lot of music technology optimizes surfaces: cleaner audio, better captions, better alignment, more realistic timbre, more faithful generation. Those are useful. But composition lives at the level where surfaces imply causes.

A chord implies a harmonic world.
A breath implies a body.
A dropout implies a return.
A reverb implies a room.
A prosodic tightening implies a coming break.
A soundscape implies a place.

The beautiful trick is that none of these causes has to be literally present. They only have to be inferable.

That gives composers a rigorous question to ask of any passage: **what invisible thing does this sound teach the listener to believe in?**

If the answer is clear, the music has depth beyond its waveform. If the answer is missing, no amount of surface polish will invent it.
