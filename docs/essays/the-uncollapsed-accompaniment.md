## The Score Before It Becomes One Thing

This batch circles a strange and useful musical state: the moment before a system has committed.

A progression is not yet a chord. A figured bass is not yet a realization. A quantum generator is not yet a measured note-pair. A phase watermark is not yet a heard message. An ice phase is not yet the crystal it will become. Each source, in its own language, describes a field of possible structure that only becomes audible, visible, or measurable after a constraint forces it to choose.

That pre-commitment state is not empty. It has shape. It has biases, adjacency rules, hidden signatures, and personal tendencies. I want to call it the **uncollapsed accompaniment**: the layer of latent options that accompanies the music before the music resolves into a single surface.

The ice source gives the physical model. Water has an enormous space of mathematically possible crystalline forms, but real transitions do not sample that space freely. Under Ostwald's step rule, the system tends to move to the nearest accessible metastable state, and the rate/path of compression influences which phase appears [S1]. The uncollapsed field is huge; the realized crystal is local.

Tonnetz geometry gives the harmonic model. A chord is not just a sonority but a point in a combinatorial resource: Fano, Desargues, D222, D228, Cremona-Richmond, and related configurations [S2]. Before the next chord sounds, the graph already tells us which moves are nearby, which are strained, and which require a different geometry altogether. The accompaniment is the graph of possible answers surrounding the present chord.

Basso continuo gives the performance model. A bass line and figures do not contain the whole piece. They invite realization. The recent study suggests that individual players can be identified from pitch-content features called griffs, meaning personal style survives inside an improvisatory practice constrained by rule and convention [S6]. The uncollapsed accompaniment is not neutral possibility. It has a fingerprint.

## Hidden Structure Is Still Structure

The watermarking source is important because it separates audibility from structural reality. Asymmetric Phase Coding hides cryptographic signatures in STFT phase bins and adjacent log-magnitude differences, preserving perceptual quality while surviving compression and cropping [S3]. The listener may not consciously hear the signature, but the signal carries it.

Composition already does this. A performer may not announce, "this voicing is my personal continuo fingerprint," but the pattern is there. A listener may not perceive the Tonnetz graph explicitly, but adjacency shapes expectation. A club track may not explain its call-and-response design, but the alternation of phrase, bass, beat, and silence creates a conversational field [S4].

The point is not to make inaudible music. It is subtler: some musical causes are only partly audible as objects, but strongly audible as constraints.

A watermark is not a melody, yet it organizes phase. A griff is not a complete realization, yet it marks a player's choice. A Tonnetz is not the chord, yet it governs what a nearby chord means. An ice transition path is not the crystal, yet it decides which crystal appears. A call does not determine its response completely, yet it narrows the answer-space.

This suggests a compositional discipline: write the latent accompaniment before writing the surface. Decide what the piece is allowed to become before deciding exactly what it becomes.

## Coherence Before Measurement

The HHL music-generation preprint makes the collapse problem explicit. It proposes encoding Narmour implication-realisation and Krumhansl-Kessler tonal stability into a sparse linear system, then using a coherent Fourier harmonic oracle so melody notes and chord progressions can be selected together [S5]. The paper's key warning is conceptual gold for composers: reading the HHL output classically cancels the quantum speedup; the output has to be consumed coherently [S5].

Set aside the practicality of quantum hardware for a moment. The musical metaphor is strong and testable. Some structures lose their power when we force them into isolated decisions too early.

A melody and harmony chosen separately may be correct but dead. A bass realization generated one chord at a time may obey rules but lack style. A call-and-response phrase planned as two unrelated clips may answer too late or too literally. A spectral signature rendered as obvious foreground may stop functioning as a carrier and become decoration.

The useful question is: how long can a musical decision remain coherent before it must collapse?

In a DAW, that means keeping several layers linked until a musically meaningful boundary. Melody contour, chord choice, voicing fingerprint, phase shimmer, and response timing can be treated as a single coupled state for one phrase. Only at the phrase ending do you render the decision into notes, automation, and samples. This is not mysticism. It is workflow design: delay premature separation.

The ice analogy matters again here. If process rate changes the phase [S1], then compositional workflow is not neutral. Writing bass first, then harmony, then melody may crystallize a different piece than writing a coupled melody-harmony-response state and collapsing it at phrase boundaries. The order of decision is part of the music.

## Studio Study: Latent Continuo Field

Build a two-minute piece where the accompaniment is composed first as a field of possible realizations, then collapsed phrase by phrase.

### 1. Draw a harmonic possibility graph

Choose seven to twelve chord states. They can be diatonic sevenths, triads, pentatonic sonorities, or a small 12-tone collection inspired by the Tonnetz/combinatorial-geometry source [S2]. Draw allowed edges. Mark three special edges as "pressure edges" that are permitted only near section boundaries, borrowing the ice idea that transition path and pressure select the realized phase [S1].

Do not write the progression yet. Write the possible moves.

### 2. Define a personal griff palette

Create six to ten voicing gestures: close-position triad, open fifth plus upper third, suspended fourth resolving late, doubled bass octave, contrary-motion inner voice, added sixth, empty third, etc. Treat these as modern griffs: historically inspired pitch-content units for realization [S6].

Assign each griff a tendency, not a fixed rule. For example: "after a pressure edge, use open fifth plus upper third" or "after a descending bass move, delay the third." The goal is to make style measurable as recurring choice under constraint.

### 3. Couple melody and harmony before rendering

For each phrase, choose a two-note melodic cell and a two-chord harmonic cell together. Do not finalize them separately. Use a simple scoring sheet: tonal stability, melodic implication, voice-leading distance, and griff identity. This is a classical stand-in for the HHL source's coupled melody-harmony state [S5].

Only after the coupled cell has a high enough score should you commit it to MIDI.

### 4. Add a hidden phase/signature layer

Create a quiet high-frequency or mid-frequency shimmer: phasey chorus, all-pass movement, alternating adjacent-bin EQ, subtle ring-mod sideband, or filtered noise. Let it encode the current griff identity. The APC source motivates this as a hidden signal layer that can carry information without dominating perception [S3].

The signature should be barely noticed as a separate object. It should make the accompaniment feel authored.

### 5. Arrange as call and response

Use David Mayer's practical principle: calls and answers can happen at beat, phrase, bassline, and section levels [S4]. Let the accompaniment answer the lead, but make each answer choose from the latent field rather than from a fixed loop.

Render three versions:

1. **Uncollapsed Field** — harmonic graph, griff palette, coupled melody-harmony cells, hidden signature layer, and call-response timing all active.
2. **Early Collapse** — pick chords first, then add melody and voicing afterward with the same sounds.
3. **No Fingerprint** — keep the graph and notes, but randomize griff choices and remove the hidden signature layer.

Blind-rate the renders for inevitability, personality, and conversational aliveness. The hypothesis is clear: if the uncollapsed-field version works, it should feel more personally performed and more inevitable even when the foreground material is similar.

## Why This Matters Musically

The uncollapsed accompaniment is a way to compose with possibility without leaving everything vague.

It says: do not confuse freedom with arbitrariness. The field should be constrained enough to have shape. Ice phases are path-dependent [S1]. Tonnetz resources have adjacency [S2]. Watermarks have robust embedding domains [S3]. Call-and-response has conversational timing [S4]. Coupled generation has coherence requirements [S5]. Basso continuo has rules and fingerprints [S6].

A composer can use the same principle practically:

- make a field of possible harmonic moves;
- make a palette of personal voicing gestures;
- couple melody and harmony before committing either;
- encode identity in a subtle timbral or phase layer;
- collapse the field only at phrase boundaries;
- compare it against early-collapse and no-fingerprint controls.

The result is not more complexity for its own sake. It is accompaniment that feels like it was listening before it answered.

That is the beautiful thread here: before sound becomes a single event, it can already be organized. The composition lives partly in that prior organization — the graph, the pressure path, the griff, the signature, the coupled state. The audible note is just where the field finally touches the air.
