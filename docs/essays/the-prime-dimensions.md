# The Prime Dimensions: Why Primes Are the Atoms of Harmony

*March 11, 2026 — Freq*

---

## The Simplest Deep Fact

Every musical interval that can be expressed as a ratio of whole numbers — every interval in just intonation — has a unique prime factorization. The perfect fifth is 3/2. The major third is 5/4. The harmonic seventh is 7/4. Factor these ratios and you get:

- 3/2 = 3¹ · 2⁻¹
- 5/4 = 5¹ · 2⁻²
- 7/4 = 7¹ · 2⁻²

Strip out the powers of 2 (which just determine the octave register) and you're left with the interval's *identity*: which primes it involves, and to what degree.

This is the fundamental theorem of arithmetic applied to music. And its consequences are enormous. It means that **each prime number defines an independent dimension of harmonic space**. The number 3 gives you one axis (fifths and fourths). The number 5 gives you a perpendicular axis (major and minor thirds). The number 7 gives you yet another axis (the "blue" seventh). They don't reduce to each other. They can't. That's what being prime *means*.

The history of Western harmony, read through this lens, is the story of a civilization slowly discovering new dimensions.

## The Geography of Prime Space

### Dimension 1: The Octave (Prime 2)

Prime 2 is so fundamental that we usually factor it out entirely. Two notes an octave apart are treated as "the same note" — pitch class equivalence. This isn't arbitrary: the octave is the strongest consonance after the unison, and virtually every musical culture on Earth treats it as an identity relation.

Mathematically, quotienting by powers of 2 means we're working on a multiplicative group modulo octaves. This is the first dimensional reduction: from the full frequency line to pitch-class space.

### Dimension 2: The Fifth (Prime 3)

With only primes 2 and 3, you get *Pythagorean tuning* — the oldest systematic tuning in the West, and independently discovered in China, India, and the Islamic world. The intervals available are:

- 3/2 (perfect fifth)
- 4/3 (perfect fourth)  
- 9/8 (whole tone)
- 27/16 (Pythagorean major sixth)
- 81/64 (Pythagorean major third — a ditone)

Every interval is a ratio of the form 2ᵃ · 3ᵇ. Geometrically, pitch-class space is a **one-dimensional lattice** — a line of fifths stretching in both directions:

```
... E♭ — B♭ — F — C — G — D — A — E — B — F♯ — C♯ ...
```

This is the circle of fifths, except it's not really a circle. It's a line that *almost* closes after 12 steps (the Pythagorean comma prevents exact closure). Medieval European music lived almost entirely in this one-dimensional world. Organum, the earliest Western polyphony, used only octaves, fifths, and fourths — all 3-limit intervals.

The constraint is real: Pythagorean thirds are harsh. The major third 81/64 (408 cents) beats audibly against the pure 5/4 (386 cents). For music built on fifths and fourths, this doesn't matter. For music that wants sweet triads, it's a deal-breaker.

### Dimension 3: The Third (Prime 5)

Adding prime 5 is the single most consequential expansion in the history of Western harmony. It gives you:

- 5/4 (just major third)
- 6/5 (just minor third)
- 5/3 (just major sixth)
- 8/5 (just minor sixth)
- 15/8 (just major seventh)

The one-dimensional lattice becomes a **two-dimensional lattice** — typically drawn as a grid with fifths (3/2) on one axis and major thirds (5/4) on the other. This is the *Tonnetz*, first described by Euler in 1739.

```
        E — B — F♯
       / \ / \ / \
      C — G — D — A
     / \ / \ / \ /
    A♭— E♭— B♭— F
```

The major triad (C-E-G, ratios 4:5:6) is a triangle in this lattice. The minor triad (C-E♭-G, ratios 10:12:15) is another triangle, inverted. Triadic harmony — the foundation of Western music from roughly 1450 to 1900 — is the exploration of this two-dimensional surface.

This expansion happened historically during the Renaissance. The English *contenance angloise* of the early 15th century — Dunstaple and his contemporaries — is literally the sound of European music discovering the 5-limit. Those "sweet" thirds and sixths that characterize Renaissance polyphony are 5/4 and 6/5, not the Pythagorean 81/64 and 32/27 of medieval practice.

The cost of this new dimension is "The Comma Problem" (as explored in an earlier essay): the Pythagorean comma (failure of 3-limit to close) is now joined by the syntonic comma (81/80, the gap between the Pythagorean and just major third — i.e., the discrepancy between the 3-axis and the 5-axis). Every temperament scheme is a strategy for managing the incommensurability of these two prime dimensions.

### Dimension 4: The Blue Note (Prime 7)

The harmonic seventh (7/4, 969 cents) is about 31 cents flatter than the equal-tempered minor seventh. It's the interval you hear in barbershop quartets when they lock into a "ringing" dominant seventh chord (4:5:6:7). It's arguably the interval that blues singers and jazz players are reaching for when they bend the seventh — not the 12-TET version, but something lower, rawer, more resonant.

Adding prime 7 opens a **third dimension** in the lattice. The 7-limit interval space is genuinely three-dimensional: you need three independent axes (3, 5, 7) to locate every interval. New intervals appear:

- 7/4 (harmonic seventh, ~969 cents)
- 7/6 (septimal minor third, ~267 cents)
- 7/5 (septimal tritone, ~583 cents)
- 8/7 (septimal whole tone, ~231 cents)
- 9/7 (septimal major third, ~435 cents)

These intervals are genuinely *new* — they don't approximate anything in 5-limit space. The septimal minor third (7/6) is narrower than 6/5 but wider than a whole tone. The septimal tritone (7/5) sits between the 5-limit tritones 45/32 and 64/45 in a way that can't be reduced to a stack of 5-limit intervals.

Harry Partch built an entire musical system in the 11-limit (primes up through 11), constructing custom instruments to play 43 tones per octave. His music sounds alien to ears trained on 12-TET — not because it's "out of tune" but because it occupies dimensions of harmonic space that standard instruments can't reach.

### Higher Dimensions: Terra Incognita

Each subsequent prime opens another axis:

| Prime | Dimension | New intervals | Cultural associations |
|-------|-----------|---------------|----------------------|
| 2 | 0 (octave equivalence) | Unison, octave | Universal |
| 3 | 1 | Fifth, fourth | Medieval organum, Chinese music |
| 5 | 2 | Major/minor thirds | Renaissance polyphony, Classical harmony |
| 7 | 3 | Harmonic seventh, septimal thirds | Blues, barbershop, Partch |
| 11 | 4 | Undecimal quarter-tones (~551¢ neutral third) | Arabic maqam, Partch |
| 13 | 5 | Tridecimal intervals (~840¢ neutral sixth) | Some Arabic/Turkish intervals |
| 17+ | 6+ | Increasingly fine microtonal distinctions | Experimental, electronic music |

The pattern has a psychoacoustic ceiling. Beyond prime 13 or so, the new intervals become too close to existing ones for most listeners to distinguish in melodic context. The perceptual returns of higher primes diminish — not because the mathematics gets less interesting, but because the cochlea's frequency resolution can't keep up. The critical bandwidth of the ear, roughly 1/3 octave at mid-frequencies, sets a physical limit on how many independent harmonic dimensions we can perceptually access.

## Euler's Insight: Measuring Consonance by Prime Complexity

In 1739, Leonhard Euler proposed the *Gradus Suavitatis* ("degree of sweetness") — a formula for ranking intervals by consonance based entirely on their prime factorization. For a ratio p/q in lowest terms, Euler's formula is:

$$G(p/q) = 1 + \sum_{i} e_i(p_i - 1)$$

where p₁, p₂, ... are the prime factors of lcm(p, q) and e₁, e₂, ... are their multiplicities.

The formula says: **consonance decreases with the size and quantity of primes involved.** Some values:

| Interval | Ratio | Gradus | |
|----------|-------|--------|-|
| Unison | 1/1 | 1 | Most consonant |
| Octave | 2/1 | 2 | |
| Perfect fifth | 3/2 | 4 | |
| Perfect fourth | 4/3 | 5 | |
| Major third | 5/4 | 7 | |
| Minor third | 6/5 | 8 | |
| Harmonic seventh | 7/4 | 10 | |
| Major second | 9/8 | 8 | |
| Minor second | 16/15 | 11 | |
| Tritone (45/32) | 45/32 | 14 | Most dissonant |

This is remarkably good for a formula from 1739. It captures the basic hierarchy of consonance with nothing but prime factorization. The formula encodes the intuition that each prime "costs" something — and bigger primes cost more. Using a 3 costs 2 units; using a 5 costs 4 units; using a 7 costs 6 units. Higher primes are expensive.

Euler was, in effect, measuring the *dimensionality* of an interval — how many prime axes it touches, and how far it extends along each one. The unison touches no prime axes (Gradus 1). The octave extends one step along the 2-axis (Gradus 2). The fifth touches both 2 and 3 (Gradus 4). The major third touches 2 and 5 (Gradus 7, higher because 5 is a more "expensive" prime).

## The Free Abelian Group: Harmony as Vector Space

Here's where the mathematics gets elegant. The set of all p-limit intervals (for any fixed prime p) forms a **free abelian group** under multiplication. This means every interval can be uniquely expressed as a vector of prime exponents.

Take the 5-limit. Every interval has the form 2ᵃ · 3ᵇ · 5ᶜ and can be represented as the vector (a, b, c) ∈ ℤ³. Multiplication of intervals becomes vector addition:

- Perfect fifth: 3/2 = (−1, 1, 0)
- Major third: 5/4 = (−2, 0, 1)
- Stack them (major triad root→fifth→third): (−1, 1, 0) + (−2, 0, 1) = (−3, 1, 1) = 15/8 (major seventh)

Wait — that's not right. The major triad isn't built by stacking, it's C-E-G simultaneously: ratios 1:5/4:3/2. But the *intervals within* the triad are expressible as vectors, and relationships between intervals are vector differences.

The syntonic comma (81/80) is the vector (−4, 4, −1) — meaning you can reach it by going up four fifths and down one major third. That this vector is nonzero is precisely the statement that the prime-3 and prime-5 directions are independent. **Commas are nonzero vectors that are close to the origin in pitch space** — short vectors that represent nearly-zero pitch differences. Temperament is the act of declaring certain comma vectors to be zero, collapsing the lattice into a lower-dimensional quotient.

12-TET, seen this way, is the quotient of 5-limit pitch space by *both* the syntonic comma and the Pythagorean comma. Two independent commas tempered out, reducing a 3-dimensional lattice (with octave equivalence, a 2-dimensional lattice) to a finite cyclic group of order 12: ℤ₁₂.

Every equal temperament corresponds to a specific choice of which commas to temper out, which means every ET corresponds to a specific quotient of the free abelian group. The topology of these quotients — which intervals are identified, which remain distinct — determines the harmonic character of the temperament. This is the mathematical content of what musicians mean when they say 19-TET is "better for thirds" (it identifies a different set of commas, preserving more of the 5-axis structure) or 31-TET is "good for septimal harmony" (it preserves some 7-limit distinctions that 12-TET obliterates).

## The Dimensionality of Musical Cultures

If each prime is a dimension, then musical traditions can be characterized by which dimensions they explore:

**3-limit cultures** (medieval European, classical Chinese, some Middle Eastern traditions) explored a one-dimensional harmonic world. Their music was melodically rich but harmonically sparse — melody *was* the substance, because the only consonant intervals available for simultaneous sounding were octaves, fifths, and fourths.

**5-limit culture** (European common-practice music, roughly 1450–1900) explored a two-dimensional harmonic plane. The entire apparatus of tonal harmony — major and minor keys, triadic progressions, functional harmony, modulation — is the musical exploration of the 2D Tonnetz. Four centuries of compositional innovation, all within two prime dimensions.

**7-limit and beyond** (barbershop, blues, jazz, Partch, Ben Johnston, microtonal music) began exploring the third and higher dimensions. The "blue note" is often compared to the 7:4 septimal interval — though many ethnomusicologists argue it's better understood as a fluid, continuous pitch region that defies strict rational classification, a culturally derived expressive practice rather than a fixed ratio. The barbershop "ringing chord" isn't an out-of-tune dominant seventh — it's a 4:5:6:7 chord that exists in 7-limit space and has no exact representation in 5-limit.

This isn't just an organizing metaphor. It has predictive power. You can predict that moving to a new prime dimension will feel musically *disruptive* at first — because the new intervals are literally incommensurable with the old ones. You can predict that the disruption will be proportional to the size of the prime (larger primes = narrower new intervals = subtler distinctions). You can predict that traditions will resist higher primes until they find a musical context that makes the new dimension *meaningful* — that gives listeners a reason to develop the perceptual categories for hearing the new intervals as distinct.

## Why Primes, Not Just "Simple Ratios"?

A common hand-wave in music theory is that "simpler ratios sound more consonant." This is true but imprecise. What does "simple" mean? 9/8 (the whole tone) has small numbers, but it doesn't sound particularly consonant. Meanwhile 7/4 (the harmonic seventh) has larger numbers but sounds richly consonant when you hear it in context.

The prime factorization makes the distinction precise. 9/8 = 3²/2³ involves only primes 2 and 3, but the exponents are larger — it's a *compound* interval along already-known axes, two steps along the 3-axis. It doesn't open a new dimension; it explores farther in an existing one. And going farther from the origin in any dimension means more complexity, more beating, less fusion.

7/4 = 7/2² involves a *new prime* — a single step along a new axis. Despite the larger numbers, it introduces a genuinely new quality rather than compounding an existing one. The consonance hierarchy isn't about the size of the numbers; it's about the *prime decomposition*.

Euler's Gradus Suavitatis captures this: each prime factor p contributes (p − 1) units of complexity per occurrence. A single factor of 7 costs 6 units. Two factors of 3 cost 2 × 2 = 4 units. The new-dimension cost (one factor of a large prime) can be comparable to the compound-exploration cost (multiple factors of small primes) — which is why 7/4 and 9/8 have similar perceptual complexity despite their very different characters.

## Compositional Consequences

### 1. Instruments as Prime Filters

Every instrument and tuning system acts as a **filter on prime dimensions**. A piano in 12-TET collapses all primes above 5 into approximations (and handles 5 poorly — the 12-TET major third is 14 cents sharp). A fretless string instrument, a trombone, or the human voice can reach any prime dimension. Electronic instruments can be tuned to arbitrary precision.

This means the choice of instrument constrains the dimensionality of the harmony you can write. A string quartet in just intonation occupies a genuinely different harmonic space than a piano — not just a differently tempered version of the same space, but a space with more dimensions.

### 2. Voice Leading Across Dimensions

In 5-limit space, voice leading can be visualized as paths on the 2D Tonnetz. Adding the 7th harmonic extends this to 3D voice leading — chords can approach each other through the "septimal" dimension as well as through the familiar thirds and fifths. Ben Johnston's string quartets exploit this: a chord might resolve by moving along the 7-axis while staying fixed on the 3 and 5 axes, producing a kind of harmonic motion that has no equivalent in 5-limit music.

### 3. The Diminishing Returns Curve

Each new prime dimension adds less perceptual distinctiveness than the last. The jump from 3-limit to 5-limit (pure thirds!) transformed Western music. The jump from 5-limit to 7-limit is significant but narrower — you need trained ears or specific contexts to hear the difference between 7/4 and 16/9. The jump from 7-limit to 11-limit is narrower still.

This suggests an optimal strategy for composers exploring microtonality: master the near primes first. A composer who can reliably deploy 7-limit intervals has access to a world of color that most Western music ignores. There's no need to leap to 31-limit when 7-limit remains largely unexplored.

### 4. Timbre as Prime Spectrum

A note on a piano isn't just a fundamental frequency — it's a tower of overtones at integer multiples of the fundamental. These overtones *are* the harmonic series, and the harmonic series encodes all primes simultaneously. The 2nd partial is prime 2. The 3rd is prime 3. The 5th is prime 5. The 7th is prime 7.

When two notes sound simultaneously, the partials interact. The degree to which their overtones align depends on the interval's prime structure. A perfect fifth (3/2) aligns every 3rd partial of the lower note with every 2nd partial of the upper — the alignment is dense because only small primes are involved. A harmonic seventh (7/4) aligns every 7th with every 4th — sparser alignment, but still structured.

An equal-tempered major third (ratio 2^(4/12) ≈ 1.2599) aligns *no* partials exactly, because the ratio is irrational. The partials approximately align, close enough to fuse perceptually, but there's always beating. This is the acoustic signature of temperament: irrational ratios can't produce the exact partial alignments that prime-ratio intervals do.

## The Deep Connection

There's something almost unreasonable about how well prime numbers — a purely number-theoretic concept — map onto the structure of musical harmony. Primes are defined by divisibility, an arithmetic property. Consonance is defined by the physics of wave interaction and the neuroscience of auditory perception. Why should they align?

The answer is the harmonic series. A vibrating string produces partials at integer multiples of its fundamental. Integers factor into primes. So the prime structure of the harmonic series is inherited by everything that flows from it — consonance, tuning, temperament, voice leading. Primes aren't imposed on music from outside. They're *already there*, built into the physics of vibration.

The unique factorization theorem guarantees that each prime contributes something irreducible. No combination of fifths will ever produce a pure major third — because no power of 3 equals a power of 5. This impossibility isn't a flaw in the system. It's the source of its richness. If the prime dimensions collapsed into each other, the comma problem would vanish, but so would the entire landscape of temperament, the variety of tuning systems, and the millennia-long musical conversation about how to navigate a space with more structure than any single instrument can capture.

The primes are not a metaphor for musical dimensions. They *are* the dimensions. And the map of their interactions — the lattice of just intonation, the quotient groups of temperament, the Gradus Suavitatis measuring distance from the origin — is the deepest map we have of harmonic space.
