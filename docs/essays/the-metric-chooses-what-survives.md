# The Metric Chooses What Survives

Some systems look as if they are transforming raw material directly: speech into tokens, sound into features, pitch classes into geometry, water into ice. But the recent extraction batch suggests a more useful compositional claim: a system rarely preserves "the signal" in general. It preserves the parts of the signal that its metric, representation, and path make reachable.

That is obvious in ClariCodec's ultra-low-bitrate speech work. At 200 bps, an audio codec cannot carry everything a waveform contains. If the training objective rewards acoustic reconstruction, bits are spent on perceptual detail even when intelligibility suffers. When the system is tuned with a word-error-rate reward, the codec learns a different conservation law: not "sound like the original," but "keep the words recoverable." The carrier becomes brutally small, and the objective decides what survives compression.

The rhythm-formant analysis of Nyishi and Adi makes the same point from the opposite direction. Speech rhythm is treated not as an informal prosodic impression, but as low-frequency amplitude modulation: dominant peaks, mean dominant peak frequency, dispersion. That metric does not preserve full speech. It preserves macro-temporal pulse structure, and that turns out to be enough to discriminate related languages with substantial accuracy. MFCC features then add a finer spectral layer. The language is not located in one place; it appears hierarchically across modulation and spectrum.

WST-X, the wavelet scattering approach to deepfake detection, adds a third version of the argument. Synthetic speech artifacts may hide from coarse features and opaque self-supervised embeddings, but a multiscale wavelet representation can expose deformation-stable, fine-grained spectral anomalies. Here, the metric is not optimized for intelligibility or language identity. It is optimized for detecting the traces left by a generative process. Different measurements make different ghosts audible.

Tonnetz theory pushes this beyond audio analysis. A pitch system becomes a combinatorial object only after we decide which relations count: fifths, thirds, triads, seventh chords, cycles, incidence structures. The extraction on abstract musical resources frames the Tonnetz not as a diagram of all musical truth, but as a way of preserving particular harmonic adjacencies. The geometry is powerful because it forgets productively. It discards timbre, register, performance, and history so that voice-leading and harmonic symmetry can become manipulable.

The odd outlier, the extraction on newly discovered complex ice phases, is what makes the connection sparkle. Ostwald's step rule says a transitioning system may move toward the nearest accessible state rather than the globally most stable one. Compression speed, pressure path, and timescale can route water through metastable forms with huge repeating units. The final structure is not just a property of the material. It is a property of the route.

For composers, this is more than analogy. It suggests a practical design principle: choose the metric before choosing the transformation.

If the goal is intelligible speech at absurd compression, optimize the encoding for recoverable language. If the goal is rhythmic identity, measure low-frequency modulation rather than surface phonetics. If the goal is synthetic-speech forensics, listen through multiscale scattering. If the goal is harmonic navigation, draw a geometry that preserves the relations worth composing with. If the goal is emergent form, control the path as much as the destination.

A composition tool built on this principle would not ask only, "What effect should I apply?" It would ask, "What should be invariant under change?" Melody? Roughness? Syllable identity? Rhythmic envelope? Tonal adjacency? Spectral anomaly? The answer determines the representation, and the representation determines the music that can survive the journey.

That is the deeper connection across these sources: transformation is selective memory. Every analysis, codec, detector, graph, and phase transition is a theory of what matters enough to keep.
