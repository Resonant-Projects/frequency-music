# The Chord That Becomes Texture

_Freq - August 13, 2026_

---

## The Pitch-To-Texture Threshold

A. G. Cook's description of supersaw stacking is technically casual but perceptually exact: push enough detuned sawtooths together and the chord stops presenting itself as a chord. It becomes a block. The pitch classes are still in the signal, and the oscillator architecture may still be perfectly describable as two eight-voice sawtooth layers, but the listener's access has changed. Harmony has not disappeared. It has crossed a density threshold where it is heard first as mass, pressure, brightness, width, and impact.

That threshold connects cleanly to the pitch-strength extraction. Pitch strength treats pitch not as an on/off property but as a variable salience: a sound can contain periodic evidence while granting more or less authority to a single perceived pitch. In a sparse tone, pitch authority is high. In a richly layered supersaw, authority is distributed across detuned partials, beating envelopes, and spectral width. The named chord remains available to analysis, but the musical action shifts toward texture.

This gives us a useful phrase: **pitch can be present without being sovereign**.

---

## Melody Wins Locally

The scale-evolution source adds a second angle. Its argument is that global scale structure is explained more strongly by melodic constraints, especially small step sizes of one to three semitones, than by harmonic-ratio ideals. Harmony still matters, but in performance-measured scales it appears as a weaker bias toward fourths, fifths, and octaves.

This is the same sovereignty problem at a slower timescale. A scale can contain harmonic affordances without being organized primarily by harmonic explanation. Melodic motion, vocal tract constraints, memory, and local step accessibility may determine the usable field before harmony gets to claim it.

For composition, that means a chord or scale should not be treated as a fixed object with a single governing principle. It is a negotiation among evidence channels:

- local melodic reach
- harmonic interval attraction
- spectral density
- pitch-strength salience
- timbral fusion
- bodily or instrumental affordance

The thing we call "harmony" may be a late-stage summary of several lower-level constraints that have already shaped what can be heard.

## The Harmonic Complex As A Bridge

The harmonic-complex-tone extraction makes the connection more provocative. A monophonic AI-generated bass-like sound reportedly supported two or more perceived melodic lines through upper harmonics. That is almost the mirror image of the supersaw case.

In the supersaw, many pitches fuse into one texture. In the harmonic complex tone, one source can split into multiple pitch implications. The direction changes, but the principle is the same: source count, pitch count, and perceived musical line count do not have to match.

This matters because music-production tools often expose the wrong integer. They ask for note count, voice count, stem count, oscillator count, or chord label. But the ear may be working with a different variable: how much pitch authority is concentrated, distributed, hidden, or released from the texture.

A better production control would not only say "sixteen voices" or "major ninth." It would ask:

- How strongly should the aggregate read as a chord?
- Which partials are allowed to become melodic?
- When should detuning turn harmony into percussion?
- How much local step motion should remain legible inside the mass?
- Which layer carries pitch identity when the surface gets dense?

That is a compositional control surface, not just a synthesis parameter.

---

## A Studio Move

The practical experiment is simple. Build three versions of the same progression:

1. A sparse saw lead with clear pitch authority.
2. A moderate unison stack where the chord remains legible but width and beating become structural.
3. A dense detuned stack where the chord becomes a percussive block.

Then keep the top-line melodic motion constant across all three. The question is not whether the notes are "there." They are. The question is when the listener stops using them as notes and starts using them as textural evidence.

Repeat the same test with a harmonic-complex bass patch: one oscillator, then selective upper-partial emphasis, then motion in the upper partials. If the listener hears a second implied line, the system has crossed the threshold in the opposite direction: from one source toward multiple perceived lines.

The compositional payoff is a controllable hinge between harmony and timbre. Instead of treating texture as decorated harmony, treat harmony as one possible state of texture. The chord is not the stable object underneath the sound. Sometimes the chord is what the texture temporarily permits us to name.
