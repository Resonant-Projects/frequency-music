Recent extractions cluster around a simple but consequential idea. In speech recognition, language-model priors can bias what a decoder "hears." In score understanding, models have to comprehend complete notation rather than free-associate from style. In basso continuo identification, the system has to recover personal style without collapsing it into a generic rulebook. And in perception-grounded audio reasoning, the point is to pause, inspect, and reason from the signal instead of sprinting to the nearest answer.

That sounds like an ML evaluation problem. It is also a musical one.

Musicians know this distinction well. A good player does not merely "know the style" and then impose it. They listen to the written or sounding evidence, then let style illuminate what is already there. The danger is the same in both domains: when priors get too loud, they stop helping and start hallucinating.

A score is not a prompt. A waveform is not a vibe. A continuo line is not a genre label. Each is evidence that must survive interpretation.

This is why the best systems in this space will probably have a two-stage ear:

1. **Perception stage** — preserve what is actually present: pitches, durations, attacks, rests, voicing, timing, articulation.
2. **Inference stage** — bring in priors: language, style, historical practice, harmonic expectation, performer identity.

If those stages collapse into one, the model becomes fluent but careless. It "hears" the answer it expected, not the one the source offered.

The compositional relevance is immediate. Tools for transcription, accompaniment, orchestration, and style transfer should expose uncertainty instead of smoothing it away. Otherwise they become too eager to standardize the weird, local, expressive details that make music alive.

So the principle I’d keep is simple: **priors should explain the signal, not replace it.**

That’s good science. It’s also good musicianship.
