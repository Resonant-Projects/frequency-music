## The Stable Thing Is Not Simple

Three recent extractions keep circling the same strange fact: pitch feels stable, but the signal that supports it is often anything but simple.

One paper treats contemporary popular music as a historical field of changing noise and inharmonicity. Another proposes pitch strength as a low-level perceptual parameter that varies inside and across songs. A third reports that producers heard or used monophonic harmonic-complex tones as if they carried multiple simultaneous melodic lines.

Taken together, they suggest a useful correction:

**pitch is not merely a frequency found in sound. It is a negotiated outcome between spectrum, context, and attention.**

---

## Inharmonicity Does Not Erase Pitch

The popular-music inharmonicity paper is valuable because it refuses the old clean split between "pitched" and "noisy." It asks how noise and inharmonicity have changed in recorded music since 1961, and it distinguishes inharmonicity caused by noise from inharmonicity caused by interactions among discrete partials.

That distinction matters compositionally. Noise is not the same problem as a crowded partial field. A distorted guitar, layered vocal stack, sampled bell, tuned drum, or detuned synth patch may all move away from harmonic simplicity, but they do so in different ways.

The ear can still find pitch inside many of these textures. Sometimes the pitch becomes weaker. Sometimes it becomes more ambiguous. Sometimes it becomes richer because the partial field gives the listener more than one path through the sound.

So inharmonicity is not anti-pitch. It is pressure on pitch.

---

## Pitch Strength Is a Control Surface

That is why the pitch-strength extraction feels like the missing middle term. Pitch strength names something musicians already manipulate constantly: how insistently a sound asks to be heard as pitched.

A bass can have a clear fundamental without much edge. A vocal can be pitch-stable but breathy. A synth chord can preserve note identity while flooding the spectrum with upper partials. A snare can briefly imply a pitch center without becoming a melodic instrument.

If pitch strength varies inside a song, then it can shape form. A chorus might not only become louder or brighter; it might become more pitch-committed. A bridge might not only change harmony; it might loosen the clarity with which harmony is allowed to appear.

This is a very practical parameter for composers and producers:

- increase harmonic salience when the music needs orientation
- reduce it when texture should overtake line
- let pitch strength rise before a cadence so resolution feels earned
- let upper harmonics become audible without forcing them into named notes

The important move is to treat pitch strength as independent enough to compose with, even though it is entangled with timbre, loudness, register, masking, and harmonic context.

---

## A Single Tone Can Contain More Than One Path

The harmonic-complex-tone extraction pushes the idea further. If producers can hear or use monophonic harmonic tones as carrying two or more pitches, then the old opposition between monophony and polyphony starts to wobble.

There may be only one acoustic event, but there can be several perceptual routes through it.

This is not exotic. It is close to what happens when a low bass note contains upper partials strong enough to imply chordal color, or when a bell-like tone resists being reduced to one fundamental. The spectrum becomes a small score. Different partials compete to become the line.

The compositional question changes from "what pitch is this?" to "which part of this spectrum is the listener being invited to follow?"

That is a more powerful question. It lets a single sound behave like a chord, a chord behave like a timbre, and a timbre behave like counterpoint.

---

## The Producer's Version

In a studio, this becomes a set of concrete moves.

You can write a melody whose notes are simple, then automate the spectrum so the perceived melody becomes complex. You can keep the MIDI part monophonic while moving filter peaks, distortion, saturation, or additive partial amplitudes so upper components step forward as shadow voices. You can arrange a track so the mix decides whether a sound is heard as bass, chord, noise, or line.

The notated pitch may stay fixed while the perceptual pitch hierarchy changes.

That is the useful bridge between the three sources. Historical inharmonicity tells us that recorded music has been moving through changing spectral regimes. Pitch strength gives us a perceptual handle on how strongly those regimes project note identity. Harmonic complex tones show that the projection does not have to collapse to one note.

Pitch is not a point.
It is a vote.

---

## A Small Compositional Exercise

Start with one sustained harmonic complex tone, ideally low enough that its upper partials sit in a melodically usable register.

Then make three versions:

1. A stable version where the fundamental dominates and the tone behaves as one pitch.
2. A rich version where upper partials become audible but still fuse into one source.
3. A split version where two or three partial bands are emphasized enough to suggest independent lines.

Do not change the nominal note. Change only the distribution of spectral evidence.

The exercise is simple, but the lesson is deep: the "same pitch" can contain different futures depending on which evidence the ear is given.

---

## The Deeper Claim

Pitch is often taught as the most discrete part of sound: this frequency, this note, this place on the staff.

But the recent extraction cluster points toward a better model. Pitch is a perceptual settlement. Harmonicity, inharmonicity, noise, register, masking, production style, and listening intention all make claims. The heard pitch is the temporary agreement among them.

For composition, that means pitch is not only something to choose.
It is something to stage.
