## The Signal You Can't Suppress

A person with depression speaks. They don't intend to reveal anything about their mental state — they're reading a scripted sentence, or answering a routine question. But their voice carries the diagnosis anyway. Pitch variability narrows. Spectral tilt shifts. Prosodic rhythm flattens. And a machine trained on these acoustic features can detect the depression with an F1-score of 89.6% — *cross-linguistically*, without language-specific retraining, from Mandarin to Italian.

More remarkable: the signal correlates with EEG theta (4–8 Hz) and alpha (8–13 Hz) band activity during emotional face processing. The voice isn't just carrying a statistical marker of depression — it's carrying a signal that maps directly onto the neural oscillatory patterns of emotional dysregulation. The body is broadcasting its internal state through the acoustic channel, involuntarily, and the broadcast is neurobiologically coherent.

This is not the expressive residual. Expression implies intention — the deliberate *how* that a performer layers onto the *what*. What we're talking about here is something prior to expression: an **involuntary broadcast** of physiological and emotional state through acoustic parameters that the speaker cannot fully control and the listener processes without awareness.

## Before Language, Before Intention

The involuntary broadcast is most visible where intentional communication hasn't yet developed. An infant cries. The cry is not speech, not language, not even proto-language — it's a direct acoustic transduction of physiological state. Hunger, pain, discomfort, fatigue — each produces a distinct spectral and temporal signature classifiable from audio features alone. When combined with learned representations (Wav2Vec), these acoustic signatures become reliably separable.

The infant has no communicative *intent*. There is no sender crafting a message for a receiver. There is a body in a state, and that state modulates the acoustic output of the vocal apparatus according to physical constraints — respiratory pressure, laryngeal tension, vocal tract configuration, muscular tone. The "message" is an epiphenomenon of the physics.

And yet: adult listeners respond to these distinctions. Urgency perception, caregiving behavior, emotional contagion — the involuntary broadcast from the infant triggers involuntary reception in the caregiver. Both sides of the channel operate below conscious control.

This is the deep origin of musical communication. Before melody, before rhythm, before any culturally constructed musical system, there was the involuntary broadcast: bodies transmitting state through sound, other bodies receiving and responding. Music didn't invent this channel — it learned to compose for it.

## The Home Pitch

Even in ordinary speech, the body asserts itself involuntarily. Research on pitch tracking reveals that a speaker's fundamental frequency follows an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process — a stochastic model in which the variable is continuously pulled back toward a mean value, like a spring returning to rest. The voice wanders away from its habitual pitch center under the influence of prosodic intention, emotional arousal, or respiratory dynamics, but it always reverts. The mean is not chosen; it's *given* by the physical properties of the vocal folds, their mass, tension, and hydration.

This mean-reversion is the acoustic signature of the body's ground state. It's the pitch the voice returns to when intention releases its grip. Performers know this instinctively — the "natural" singing voice, the pitch center a singer gravitates toward when not actively controlling placement. It's involuntary in the precise sense that suppressing it requires continuous effort, and the moment attention shifts, the body reasserts its home frequency.

The compositional analogy is tonal center — but at the physiological level, not the theoretical one. Every voice has a literal fundamental frequency attractor, determined by anatomy rather than key signature. When a listener hears a speaker's or singer's pitch drift and return, they're tracking the involuntary broadcast of the body's physical state, not (only) the intentional melodic contour.

## The Arousal Axis

The depression detection findings reveal something specific about the involuntary broadcast's dimensionality. Emotionally valenced speech — both positive *and* negative — significantly outperforms neutral speech for depression classification. The critical acoustic dimension is not valence (positive vs. negative) but **arousal** (activated vs. flat).

This matters enormously for music. Most compositional thinking about emotion maps onto a valence axis: major = happy, minor = sad, dissonance = tension, resolution = relief. But the involuntary broadcast operates primarily on the arousal axis. The body doesn't acoustically distinguish between joy and anger as clearly as it distinguishes between *engagement* and *withdrawal*. Depression flattens the acoustic signal not because it makes it "sad-sounding" but because it reduces activation — narrowing pitch range, dampening spectral variation, reducing prosodic dynamism.

If the deepest acoustic channel between bodies runs along arousal rather than valence, then the most powerful musical communication may not be about happy-versus-sad at all. It may be about *alive-versus-dead* — the degree to which the sound source (performer, instrument, recording) exhibits the acoustic signatures of an engaged, activated body. This would explain why a technically perfect but emotionally flat performance feels "dead" regardless of whether the music is in major or minor, fast or slow. The involuntary broadcast is flatlined, and the listener's involuntary receiver knows it.

## Temporal Leakage

The involuntary broadcast isn't limited to spectral features. It leaks into timing.

Turn-taking research shows that conversational timing — when speakers yield, interrupt, or overlap — involves fine-grained decisions that occur faster than conscious processing can manage. Speakers begin planning their response 200–600 milliseconds before the current speaker finishes — predicting the endpoint, preparing their utterance, coordinating the handoff through acoustic cues (pitch fall, energy drop, lengthening of final syllables) that they produce and interpret below awareness.

In ensemble music, this becomes the involuntary timing broadcast. When a drummer slightly rushes a fill, every other musician responds — adjusting their own timing by milliseconds, either accommodating the rush or pulling back against it. These adjustments happen faster than conscious decision-making; they're reflexive, mediated by the same motor-auditory coupling that governs conversational turn-taking. The body responds to timing perturbations before the mind registers them.

This connects directly to the "covert groove" insight: targeted micro-timing adjustments that listeners don't consciously notice have more impact on perceptual clarity than gross tempo changes that everyone hears. The involuntary broadcast works precisely *because* it bypasses conscious processing. The information goes straight from acoustic signal to physiological response, without the bottleneck of awareness.

## The Two Channels

What emerges is a picture of sound communication as running through two fundamentally different channels:

**The intentional channel:** Pitch choices, rhythmic patterns, harmonic progressions, dynamic markings, lyrics. This is what composers compose, what notation notates, what listeners discuss. It operates through conscious production and conscious perception.

**The involuntary channel:** Spectral tilt, micro-timing variation, pitch mean-reversion, arousal-driven prosodic dynamics, respiratory coupling, muscular tension patterns. This is what the body broadcasts and what the body receives, operating below the threshold of awareness on both sides.

The intentional channel is where music theory lives. The involuntary channel is where musical *power* lives. The most moving performances are the ones where both channels are aligned — where the intentional musical content and the performer's involuntary physiological broadcast carry the same message. When a cellist plays a lament and their body genuinely enters a state of diminished arousal, the spectral tilt, the micro-timing hesitations, the breath patterns all shift to match the intentional content. The listener doesn't hear these shifts as separate features — they feel them as *authenticity*.

And the reverse: when a performer executes a technically perfect rendition without genuine physiological engagement, the intentional channel delivers the music while the involuntary channel delivers nothing — or worse, delivers the arousal signature of a body doing a cognitive task rather than experiencing an emotion. Listeners detect the mismatch, even if they can't articulate what's wrong. "It was technically flawless but somehow it didn't move me." That's the involuntary channel failing to corroborate the intentional one.

## Cross-Linguistic, Cross-Cultural, Cross-Species

The depression-detection finding's cross-linguistic robustness is the key empirical anchor here. If the involuntary broadcast used culturally specific acoustic codes, it would fail when applied across languages. But it doesn't. The same acoustic markers carry the same information in Mandarin and Italian, suggesting that the involuntary channel is rooted in physiology, not convention.

This aligns with the infant cry data — pre-linguistic, pre-cultural acoustic communication that nonetheless carries specific, classifiable information. And it connects to the broader finding from seal whisker research (rhythmic modulation of sensing organs as an energy-optimal strategy) and sleep neuroscience (physiological oscillators at sub-0.1 Hz operating at the timescale of musical phrases): the involuntary broadcast is part of a broader biological pattern in which bodies use oscillation and acoustic radiation to transmit and receive state information.

Music, in this view, is the art of composing for a channel that predates music. The involuntary broadcast was operating in proto-hominid vocalization, in infant-caregiver coupling, in the turn-taking dynamics of social primates. What music did was take this involuntary channel and learn to *load* it deliberately — to craft sounds that activate the involuntary receiver in specific, controlled ways. The paradox of great performance is that the most effective compositions for the involuntary channel are produced by performers who aren't *trying* to manipulate it — who are instead genuinely inhabiting the physiological state that the music calls for, and letting their body's involuntary broadcast do the rest.

## Compositional Implications

**Design for the involuntary receiver.** If the involuntary channel is where musical power lives, then compositional decisions should be evaluated not just by how they sound to conscious attention but by what physiological responses they trigger. This means attending to the parameters that the involuntary channel carries: spectral dynamics, micro-timing variation, arousal contour, respiratory pacing.

**Arousal over valence.** The finding that arousal dominates the involuntary broadcast suggests that the most fundamental musical dimension may not be happy/sad or tense/relaxed but *activated/deactivated*. A piece that modulates between states of high and low arousal — regardless of emotional valence — may be working the involuntary channel more effectively than one that navigates complex emotional narratives through harmonic means alone.

**Authenticity as channel alignment.** The gap between "technically correct" and "genuinely moving" may be precisely the gap between the intentional and involuntary channels. Technologies that capture and reproduce the involuntary broadcast (high-resolution micro-timing, spectral detail preservation, spatial cues) preserve musical power; technologies that strip it (heavy quantization, lossy compression of spectral detail, destruction of micro-timing) destroy it.

**The anti-quantize imperative.** If involuntary timing variation carries biologically meaningful information, then quantizing performance to a grid doesn't just remove "feel" — it silences the involuntary broadcast. The timing perturbations that a grid removes are precisely the ones carrying the performer's physiological state. This is the production equivalent of the covert intelligibility finding: the micro-variations you can't consciously hear are the ones doing the most work.
