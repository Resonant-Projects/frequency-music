A note is not always one vote.

The recent extraction set keeps returning to a stranger possibility: a single sound can contain several claims about pitch, and those claims can change strength without the nominal note changing. The fundamental may name the tone. Upper harmonics may begin to act like secondary voices. Noise may weaken pitch authority without erasing contour. Inharmonic partials may keep enough structure to be composed, even when they no longer behave like clean harmonic resonance.

The useful concept is **partial authority**: the degree to which a component of a spectrum is allowed to behave as musical evidence.

This is not just timbre. Timbre describes the character of the sound as a whole. Partial authority asks which parts of the spectrum can carry action: which partials can imply a line, which noisy bands can resist pitch, which upper harmonics can become salient enough to shape dissonance, and which spectral structures a model or listener treats as controllable.

## Pitch Strength Is Distributed

The pitch-strength extraction makes the first move. It proposes pitch strength as a low-level perceptual parameter for contemporary popular music, one that varies across and inside songs, contributes to structure, helps manage polyphonic dissonance, and may involve upper harmonics becoming audible under conditions of perceptual richness.

That means pitch is not only present or absent. It has force.

A sound can keep its pitch label while changing how strongly that label governs the listener's attention. A bass note can be clear enough to anchor harmony, then soften into texture. A distorted vocal can preserve contour while letting its formants, breath, and upper partials pull against the fundamental. A synth pad can keep the same MIDI notes while its spectral energy shifts the chord from a readable progression into a colored surface.

For a composer, this is a control lane. Do not only automate filter cutoff, brightness, or loudness. Automate how much authority the pitch-bearing evidence has.

## One Tone Can Carry Counterpoint

The harmonic-tones extraction pushes the idea further. In a studio-lab experiment, producers reportedly used monophonic bass-like harmonic complex tones to communicate two or more perceived pitches at once. The claim is not merely that the tones had rich spectra. It is that upper partials became musically usable enough to imply simultaneous melodic lines.

That is partial authority in its most direct form. A single source can distribute voice-leading across its spectrum. The "note" is no longer a point. It is a stack of negotiable pitch claims.

This suggests a practical design for synthesis: expose upper-partial salience as counterpoint control. Let a musician draw which harmonic regions are allowed to behave as voices over time. A monophonic line could open internal routes: first a stable residue pitch, then a fifth-like upper path, then a brighter partial trace that answers the fundamental.

The result would not be ordinary polyphony. It would be internal counterpoint, where the source remains one body but its spectrum begins to speak with more than one authority.

## Noise Is Not The Opposite Of Structure

The popular-music inharmonicity extraction complicates the simple harmonic/noisy split. It distinguishes inharmonicity associated with noise from inharmonicity produced by interactions among discrete partials, and reports historical changes in both noise and inharmonicity across popular music from 1961 to 2020.

This distinction matters. Broadband noise can reduce pitch authority by spreading evidence across too many unrelated frequencies. But discrete inharmonic partials can still be structured. They may resist the harmonic series while remaining stable enough to form a musical material.

So the compositional question is not "harmonic or noisy?" It is:

**Which spectral components have enough coherence to count?**

A prepared piano, distorted guitar, FM bell, metallic percussion sample, or overdriven vocal can all occupy different points in this space. Some components support pitch. Some support attack. Some support color. Some support instability. Partial authority lets us talk about these roles without forcing every sound into either pure tone or noise.

## Plastic Sounds Need Handles

The trajectory-variance birdsong extraction adds a developmental clue. More plastic zebra-finch vocalizations reportedly tend to have lower spectral flatness: they are more tonal and spectrally structured. The finding is modest and species-specific, but the musical implication is fertile.

Learnable change may require handles.

If a vocalization is too flat spectrally, there may be fewer stable features for a developmental system to vary, preserve, and recognize. If it has tonal structure, it offers partials, contours, and spectral anchors that can move through time while remaining identifiable.

For composition, that suggests a useful paradox: the sound most capable of transformation may not be the most chaotic sound. It may be the sound with enough internal authority to survive being changed.

## A Studio Exercise

Take one monophonic phrase and make four versions without changing the MIDI pitches:

1. Fundamental authority: the fundamental dominates and the line behaves like ordinary melody.
2. Upper authority: selected harmonics become salient enough to imply secondary motion.
3. Inharmonic authority: stable non-harmonic partials pull the note toward metallic or synthetic color.
4. Noise authority: broadband energy weakens pitch and turns the line toward texture.

Then automate transitions among those states. The musical form should come from moving authority around the spectrum, not from changing the written notes.

This gives a clean test for future tools: can the system separate pitch label from pitch authority? Can it let a composer keep "the same note" while deciding which spectral evidence is allowed to act?

When the answer is yes, a tone stops being a static object. It becomes a small politics of partials: a place where fundamentals, harmonics, inharmonic components, and noise negotiate what the listener is allowed to hear as music.
