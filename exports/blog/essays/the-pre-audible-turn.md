## What Moves Before the Music Moves?

Some changes are heard because they happen in front of us. A chord arrives. A voice enters. A beat drops. A filter opens.

But this batch keeps pointing toward a quieter possibility: many musical arrivals are prepared by a layer that is not the foreground event. It is the carrier. It moves first, often below attention, and by the time the obvious change happens the ear has already been taught how to receive it.

Ice is the clearest non-musical example. Under extreme pressure, water does not necessarily jump to the most stable crystalline structure. It can pass through nearby metastable forms because those are reachable from the current conditions. The path, rate, and timescale help determine what structure appears [S1]. That is a beautiful warning for composition: the destination is not enough. The route makes the destination possible.

Tonnetz theory says something similar in harmonic language. A chord is not only a label; it belongs to a geometry of adjacencies, configurations, and graph walks [S2]. A modulation can therefore be prepared by moving through the neighborhood around the arrival before declaring the arrival itself.

Speech representation adds a timbral version. In self-supervised speech models, pitch, intensity, F2/formant information, noise, and higher-frequency characteristics can appear as largely separable dimensions [S3]. If those axes can be moved independently, then a transition does not have to be one simultaneous edit. Brightness can begin first. A formant can lean ahead. Noise can announce a new state while pitch stays still.

The pre-audible turn is the thing that changes before the thing we think changed.

## Carriers Are Not Always Objects

A pre-audible cue is not necessarily a melody, chord, or drum hit. It may be a pressure cue, a spectral drift, a graph adjacency, a streaming window, or a sweep.

The infrasound source is speculative about hauntings and perception, so it should not be treated as strong evidence for supernatural or physiological claims. Still, it usefully marks a compositional category: energy below ordinary attention can shape experience without becoming the named musical object [S4]. Used safely and modestly, a low sub layer can function less like a bass note and more like an environmental condition.

The LoRa source offers an engineering analogy. Chirp spread spectrum sends information through frequency-sweeping carriers [S5]. That does not mean a musical chirp should be treated like a radio protocol. But it does suggest a practical gesture: a narrowband sweep can carry the listener across a transition while the motif remains unchanged.

UAF contributes the time grid. Its full-duplex audio system processes streaming sound in fixed 600 ms chunks and encodes both semantic and control signals [S6]. Again, the point is not to imitate the model. The point is to compose with small windows of preparation. Six seconds of transition can become ten 600 ms steps, each one moving a support parameter just enough for the next state to become plausible.

A pre-audible turn is a pre-arrival infrastructure.

## The Foreground Can Stay Still

The most interesting part is that the foreground may not need to change early.

Imagine a simple motif repeating for thirty-six seconds. In the control version, everything changes at barline: harmony, brightness, width, sub layer, density. It is clean, but it may feel edited.

In the pre-audible version, the motif stays fixed until the same barline. But six seconds earlier, hidden variables begin to move:

1. a quiet sub or low-frequency pressure layer rises;
2. a narrow noise band sweeps upward like a chirp;
3. one inner voice walks through a local harmonic adjacency while the outer voices remain still;
4. a formant or brightness control drifts independently from pitch;
5. stereo width opens by tiny steps;
6. the final arrival happens only after the carrier field has made it reachable.

This is Ostwald's step rule as a studio habit: do not ask only where the music should go. Ask what nearby state it can reach first [S1]. It is also a Tonnetz habit: do not ask only what chord arrives. Ask which edge the ear has to traverse [S2]. And it is a signal-processing habit: do not bundle pitch, formant, noise, and intensity into one global switch if they can be articulated as distinct dimensions [S3].

The compositional effect, if it works, is not surprise. It is inevitability.

The arrival feels less like a cut because part of the room was already turning toward it.

## A Small Test

Make two one-minute studies.

**Control A:** switch all parameters together at 0:36.

**Pre-Audible B:** begin carrier movement at 0:30, then let the foreground arrival happen at 0:36.

Use ten 600 ms ticks during the preparation window [S6]. On each tick, change one support value: sub level, chirp center frequency, formant/brightness, stereo width, or inner-voice pitch. Keep tempo, foreground motif, final harmony, and loudness target the same.

If using the infrasound idea, be careful. Treat it as a low-level compositional cue, not a physiological claim. If the playback system cannot reproduce 18–20 Hz safely and reliably, use an audible 40 Hz substitute. The experiment is about carrier timing, not about proving sub-audible effects [S4].

Then listen blind.

Ask three questions:

1. Which version makes the arrival feel more inevitable?
2. Which version feels less like an edit?
3. Which version preserves clearer direction through the transition?

The hypothesis fails if the pre-audible version feels merely smeared, vague, or no more coherent than the simultaneous switch. A pre-audible cue is not decoration. It has to prepare.

## Why This Matters

This connection feels useful because it gives composers a middle layer between structure and surface.

Traditional arrangement advice often talks about foreground objects: add a counter-melody, change the chord, introduce percussion, automate the filter. The pre-audible turn asks a different question:

**Which low-salience variable can move first so the foreground change becomes believable?**

That could be harmonic adjacency, sub pressure, spectral tilt, formant drift, stereo width, noise density, or rhythmic microtiming. The key is that it prepares the conditions of arrival without announcing itself as the main event.

In physics, path determines phase. In harmony, adjacency determines reachable motion. In speech models, dimensions can separate. In communication systems, carriers move information. In streaming audio systems, control unfolds in chunks.

In music, maybe arrival is not a point.

Maybe arrival is what happens when the carrier has already made a place for it.
