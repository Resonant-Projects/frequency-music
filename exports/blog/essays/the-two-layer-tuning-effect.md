## One Harmony, Two Kinds Of Evidence

Tuning changes more than pitch height, but it does not change every musical fact at once.

That is the useful separation in the cached synthesis on adaptive just intonation, phi-octave algebraic tunings, 12-tone equal temperament, tritone stress, Tonnetz geometry, and line-of-fifths cognition. A chord progression has at least two layers of evidence. One is local and acoustic: partial alignment, beating, roughness, difference tones, the narrow physical behavior of intervals inside a sonority. The other is structural and path-based: the route a harmony takes through a space of possible moves, the contour of voice leading, the listener's learned sense that this chord follows from that one.

Those layers interact, but they are not identical. The same harmonic traversal can be rendered in different tuning systems and remain recognizably the same path while feeling very different under the fingers and in the ear. Conversely, a locally smoother sonority can feel less continuous if the path that produced it no longer reads as the same musical motion.

This is the two-layer tuning effect: tuning can strongly alter local consonance while leaving path identity partly intact.

## The Acoustic Layer

The acoustic layer is where ratio behavior matters most directly. Just-intoned intervals align partials in simple ways. Equal temperament distributes errors so that modulation and transposition remain practical. Meantone systems spend their error differently, sweetening some thirds while making remote regions dangerous. Golden-ratio algebraic tunings and other non-octave or phi-octave systems ask an even stranger question: what happens when the generative arithmetic of the scale changes the available consonance landscape?

At this layer, small frequency differences become physical. They create beating. They shift combination tones. They decide whether a tritone feels like a smooth hinge, a rough seam, or an unstable bright pressure. A listener may not name the cents deviation, but the body of the sound changes.

That is why the synthesis proposes a tritone stress window. The tritone is a fault line: historically, psychoacoustically, and geometrically. In one tuning arm it may spread roughness across voices; in another it may focus roughness into a particular register or partial collision. If tuning has a measurable local effect, tritone-rich bars should expose it.

Compositionally, this means the tritone is not just an interval class. It is an acoustic diagnostic. It tells the composer where the tuning system spends its compromises.

## The Path Layer

The second layer is not less real; it is just less reducible to one interval's beating pattern.

Tonnetz and neo-Riemannian descriptions treat harmonic motion as traversal. The identity of a progression can live in adjacency, transformation, common-tone retention, inversional relation, and voice-leading distance. A line-of-fifths account gives another path: not the exact acoustic size of each fifth, but the learned organization of pitch classes along a cyclic or near-cyclic route.

When a fixed MIDI arrangement is rendered in several tunings, the path layer is the part that stays deliberately constant. The same notes trigger, the same voices move, the same bars pivot, the same formal windows unfold. That shared path can preserve continuity even when the acoustic layer diverges.

This suggests a practical distinction. Roughness ratings should move with tuning. Continuity ratings should move more slowly, because continuity is partly carried by the path. If continuity changes as much as roughness, then the tuning has not merely recolored the route; it has damaged the route's recognizability.

That is a beautiful compositional control surface: local acoustic pressure and global harmonic continuity can be pulled apart.

## Dynamic Tuning As A Moving Compromise

Adaptive just intonation makes the split especially sharp. A static temperament chooses its compromises before the piece begins. Adaptive JI keeps renegotiating local interval fit as the harmony moves. During rapid modulation, that can reduce roughness inside each chord, but it also risks making the pitch field feel slippery if retunings become too audible or if reference tones drift faster than short-term intonation memory can stabilize.

So adaptive tuning is not simply "more consonant." It is a moving contract between the acoustic layer and the path layer.

A slow chorale might let each chord bloom into local purity. A fast modulation chain might need enough smoothing to keep pitch memory from noticing that the ground moved. A tritone-heavy passage might intentionally refuse smoothing so the retuning action itself becomes the expressive event: the harmony does not arrive at dissonance; it retunes into dissonance.

The musical question is not whether one tuning is best. It is which layer the piece asks the listener to trust.

## Studio Protocol

A useful test is straightforward.

Write one 36-bar study at 82 BPM, in 4/4, with three twelve-bar windows:

1. **Diatonic corridor**: constrained fifths-range material to establish baseline stability.
2. **Tritone stress window**: repeated half-diminished, dominant, or symmetric pivot material.
3. **Symmetry loop**: a repeated transformational path with surface revoicing.

Render the identical MIDI, instruments, dynamics, and mix in three or more tuning arms:

- adaptive just intonation,
- static quarter-comma meantone,
- 12-tone equal temperament,
- optionally a phi-octave algebraic tuning such as Sbeta5 or SN.

Then blind-rate each window for consonance, roughness/beating, and harmonic continuity. The prediction is not that one tuning wins globally. The prediction is subtler: consonance and roughness should separate by tuning arm, especially in the tritone window, while continuity should remain more stable where the underlying path is unchanged.

If that happens, the piece has revealed two different carriers of harmonic identity. One carrier is acoustic fit. The other is path memory.

## Writing With The Split

This opens a compositional technique: assign form to one layer and drama to the other.

A passage can keep its Tonnetz path constant while moving between tunings, making the listener hear the same route under different acoustic weather. Or it can keep the tuning constant while changing path geometry, making local sonorities sound stable as the large-scale identity slips away. The tritone can serve as a pressure gauge. Adaptive retuning can become a modulation effect. Meantone wolves can become formal borders. Equal temperament can become the neutral corridor that lets the path speak without local purity taking over.

The deeper lesson is that harmony is not a single object. It is a braid of measurements: ratio, roughness, learned category, voice-leading path, register, timbre, and memory window. Tuning chooses how those measurements disagree.

That disagreement is not a problem to solve once. It is material.
