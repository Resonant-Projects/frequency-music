The newest extraction cluster keeps pointing at a subtle but powerful musical idea: a sound is not only an event. It also contains addresses.

SceneBind makes this explicit in space. It asks an audio-visual-language model not just to say what is present in a scene, but where each object is, how it relates to other objects, and how uncertain that spatial claim is. The important move is the binding of semantics to position. "A bell" is not enough; the model wants "a bell over there, probably behind that object, with this much uncertainty." For composition, that suggests a musical scene where materials are not merely labeled by timbre or source, but by their spatial commitments.

The scale-evolution extraction makes the same move in a different coordinate system. The authors argue that global scale structure is driven more by melodic step behavior than by harmonic ideals. A scale, in this view, is not primarily a vertical consonance machine. It is a navigable address system for melodic motion, with 1-3 semitone steps acting like locally reachable coordinates. Fourths, fifths, and octaves still matter, but in performance-measured scales they appear more like weak landmarks than the whole map.

The separation-first watermarking paper adds a third layer: identity that survives being mixed and separated. A naive watermark may withstand generic distortions but fail under source separation, because separation artifacts are not generic damage. They are damage produced by a particular act of re-addressing the mixture into stems. The successful version makes the separator part of the watermarking system. The address has to be designed for the route it will later travel.

Taken together, these sources suggest a useful compositional principle:

**A musical property becomes operational when it can be recovered after transformation.**

Spatial location is operational if it survives cross-modal scene binding. Scale degree is operational if it supports melodic movement across actual performance practice. Stem identity is operational if it can be recovered after mixing and source separation. In each case, the system is not asking for essence. It is asking for a trace that remains usable after the sound has moved through a medium.

That reframes a familiar compositional question. Instead of asking "what is this sound?", ask:

- What address does this sound carry?
- Which transformations preserve that address?
- Which transformations erase it, blur it, or move it somewhere else?

This can become a concrete sketching method. Write three families of material:

1. **Spatial addresses**: sounds whose identity depends on where they appear, move, or fail to localize.
2. **Melodic addresses**: lines constrained by short reachable steps, with occasional larger landmarks treated as orientation points rather than goals.
3. **Stem addresses**: layers that carry hidden identifiers, recurring features, or spectral fingerprints designed to remain recognizable after mixing, filtering, or separation.

Then process the piece by repeatedly scrambling the surface while preserving one address at a time. A passage might lose timbral clarity but keep location. Another might lose harmonic implication but keep stepwise melodic reachability. Another might dissolve into a mixture, then reappear because its stem identity was embedded deeply enough to survive.

The interesting musical tension is that these addresses do not have equal strength. SceneBind foregrounds spatial uncertainty. The scale model weakens harmony's claim to be the master coordinate system. The watermarking paper shows that robustness in one distortion regime does not guarantee robustness in another. So the composition should not treat address as a fixed label. It should treat it as a wager: this is what I think will still be recoverable later.

That wager is the expressive material. A melody can be written as a path through locally reachable intervals. A spatial mix can be written as a field of uncertain objects. A multitrack texture can be written as a set of identities that either survive or fail under separation. The listener hears not just sounds, but the changing evidence for where those sounds belong.
