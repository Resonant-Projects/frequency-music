The new proof-complexity extraction is not about music, but it gives a useful name to something musical systems do all the time.

Some mathematical statements may be true in principle while requiring proofs too long to ever write down. They are not logically unreachable. They are practically unreachable. For any working agent with finite time, memory, and attention, that difference matters less than we might want. A truth that cannot be established within the available budget does not function like usable knowledge.

Listening has the same problem.

A source may really be present in a mixture. A meter may really organize the surface. A tonal center may really be implied by the voice-leading. A room may really leave enough acoustic trace to reconstruct its shape. But if the listener, model, performer, or analyst cannot establish that fact before action is required, the structure becomes effectively inaudible.

Effective audibility is the condition where a musical or acoustic relation is not merely present, but provable enough to guide listening.

## The Proof Is In The Signal

SR-CorrNet gives the cleanest signal-processing version. Its critique of late-split speech separation is that speaker disentanglement loses force when it is postponed until the end of the architecture. The mixed signal contains spatio-spectro-temporal correlations, but those correlations have to be converted into separation filters while they still have discriminatory power.

In other words, the source is not enough. The proof of the source has to arrive in time.

FSD50K-Solo turns the same pressure into dataset curation. A clip may carry a label, but if background interference or overlapping events make the sourcehood ambiguous, the label is too cheap. The curation system synthesizes clean single-class events and controlled mixtures because it needs a practical test for whether a recording functions as one source. Single-source status is not only an ontology. It is an audibility claim.

The streaming SpeechLLM extraction makes the timing explicit. Translation must happen before the full utterance is available, so the model has to decide when partial acoustic context is sufficient. That is a proof-length problem in miniature. Waiting longer buys more evidence, but the application cannot afford an infinite proof. The useful output is the one whose justification fits inside the latency window.

## When Existing Is Not Enough

This is where the proof-complexity analogy becomes compositionally sharp.

An interval can be tuned to a beautiful ratio, but if the orchestration masks the beating pattern, the ratio is effectively inaudible. A process can generate a canon, but if the entrances are too dense or too transformed, the canon may exist more in the score than in experience. A spectral relationship can bind two sounds, but if the attack, register, or room destroys the evidence, the relation stops behaving as a relation for the listener.

That does not mean the hidden structure is worthless. It means we should stop confusing formal existence with perceptual availability.

Composers already work with this distinction intuitively. Sometimes a structure is meant to be heard as such. Sometimes it is meant to pressure the surface without becoming nameable. Sometimes it is a private scaffold that shapes decisions but never asks for recognition. Effective audibility gives us a way to ask which role a structure is playing.

## A Practical Scale

A musical idea can be placed along a simple scale:

- **Explicitly audible:** the listener can establish the relation quickly and use it while listening.
- **Eventually audible:** the relation becomes clear only after repetition, context, or retrospective memory.
- **Effectively inaudible:** the relation exists, but the required evidence exceeds the listener's practical window.
- **Operationally absent:** changing the hidden relation would not change the perceived or performed result.

That last category is important. The Quanta extraction notes that a theoretical flaw can be operationally irrelevant if no one can prove it enough to exploit it. Music has the mirror image: a theoretical structure can be operationally irrelevant if no one can hear it enough to use it.

The point is not to demand obviousness. The point is to compose the proof conditions.

## Compositional Use

Take one formal relation: a justly tuned 7:4 interval, a rhythmic palindrome, a hidden cantus firmus, a shared impulse response, a recurring formant contour, or a spatial source trajectory.

Write four versions.

In the first, make the evidence abundant. Clear register, clean onset, repetition, minimal masking, and stable context should let the relation become explicitly audible.

In the second, let the proof take time. Delay one cue. Require memory. Let the listener suspect the relation before they can confirm it.

In the third, keep the relation formally intact but bury the evidence. The structure should still organize the writing, but it should no longer function as a graspable percept.

In the fourth, change or remove the hidden relation while preserving the surface. If nothing important changes, the relation was not doing audible work. If the surface loses coherence, then the relation was acting indirectly, even without becoming explicit.

This gives a studio test for theoretical claims. Not "is the structure there?" but "what kind of proof does the structure offer, and who can afford to complete it?"

## Why This Matters

The recent extraction cluster keeps returning to limited proof. Speech separation needs early correlations. Dataset curation needs sourcehood tests. Streaming translation needs enough context before latency breaks the interaction. ASR evaluation warns that a metric can look successful while using context in a way that hides acoustic differences. The proof-complexity source gives the abstract form: practical knowledge is bounded by the cost of establishing it.

For music, that boundary is not a nuisance. It is one of the most beautiful compositional materials we have.

We can write a harmony that proves itself immediately, one that proves itself late, one that remains a private theorem, and one that cannot be proven at all from the sounding surface. Each creates a different kind of attention.

The question is not only what structure the piece contains.

The question is whether the piece gives the listener enough proof to hear it.
