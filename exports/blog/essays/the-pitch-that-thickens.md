Pitch is not a yes-or-no property. It thickens.

Four recent extraction candidates point at the same hidden variable from different angles. A study of **inharmonicity and noisiness in contemporary popular music** separates noise-based inharmonicity from inharmonicity caused by interactions among discrete partials, then suggests that studio production has made popular music persistently more inharmonic than resonance-bound orchestral music. A **generative music studio-lab** reports that producers used bass-like harmonic complex tones to convey two or more pitches at once. A paper on **pitch strength** proposes it as a low-level perceptual parameter that varies within and across popular songs, shapes structure, helps manage polyphonic dissonance, and may involve upper harmonics becoming audible. A **birdsong plasticity** paper finds that more developmentally plastic vocalizations tend to have lower spectral flatness: more tonal, more structured spectra.

The shared concept is **pitch thickness**.

Pitch thickness is the degree to which a sound retains a pitch center while accumulating internal spectral routes, partial conflicts, or latent melodic affordances. A thin pitch points cleanly to one fundamental. A thick pitch can still anchor the ear, but it carries extra addressable structure inside itself.

## The Middle Between Tone And Noise

The popular-music inharmonicity paper is useful because it refuses a lazy binary. Inharmonicity can come from noise, but it can also come from discrete partials interacting in ways that disturb harmonic alignment without dissolving into broadband texture. That distinction matters compositionally. A distorted guitar, a stacked vocal, a sampled piano, and a synthetic bass may all move away from harmonic purity, but they do not move away in the same direction.

The generative harmonic-tone experiment gives the perceptual counterexample. A single bass-like harmonic complex tone can be used by producers as if it contains more than one pitch. That does not mean every listener hears stable polyphony inside one tone. The claim should stay cautious. But it suggests a productive zone where the spectrum is still coherent enough to behave like pitch and rich enough to imply more than one line.

Pitch strength gives that zone a possible control surface. Instead of asking only "what is the pitch?" a production system could ask "how strongly does this spectrum behave as pitch right now?" That question is closer to how musicians actually work. A bass can have a strong center but weak internal clarity. A bright upper harmonic can become audible enough to pull against the fundamental. A dense chorus can keep the chord progression legible while smearing the local pitch evidence.

The birdsong plasticity result adds a surprising biological echo. In these zebra-finch data, learned and more plastic vocalizations tend to be more tonal and structured, not flatter and noisier. That suggests a beautiful inversion: the sounds most available for learning may be the ones with enough spectral order to support transformation. Plasticity does not require shapelessness. It may require a structure that can be moved.

## A Compositional Parameter

For a composer, pitch thickness would be a practical knob, not a metaphor.

At low thickness, pitch behaves like a clean coordinate. It is useful for exposed melody, tuning experiments, intonation comparisons, and any moment where the listener must track exact intervallic motion.

At medium thickness, pitch becomes a bundle. The fundamental still orients the ear, but upper harmonics, inharmonic partials, distortion products, or formant-like features create secondary cues. This is the zone where a monophonic bass timbre might imply counterline, where a vocal vowel might carry a hidden harmonic tension, or where a guitar note's production route becomes audible without changing the note name.

At high thickness, pitch starts to become texture. The sound may still have a center, but its inner spectrum becomes more important than its label. This is where stacked partials, multitracking, detuning, and processing turn harmony into surface pressure.

That gives a studio experiment:

1. Synthesize or record a sustained bass pitch in three versions: clean harmonic, harmonic with emphasized upper partials, and harmonic with controlled inharmonic partial offsets.
2. Keep the fundamental, loudness, and envelope constant.
3. Ask listeners or an analysis model to mark pitch strength, perceived secondary pitches, roughness, and line clarity.
4. Use the same material in a short phrase and automate thickness as form: thin for declarative notes, medium for implied counterpoint, high for texture.

The important control is to separate noisiness from partial interaction. If the sound thickens only by adding noise, the pitch may blur. If it thickens by moving discrete partials, it may grow extra melodic handles.

## Why This Belongs In The Graph

Frequency already has concepts like **the chord that becomes texture**, **the structured tone**, **the pitch field**, **the density horizon**, **the metric that listens back**, and **the voice between domains**. Pitch thickness links them into a more testable chain.

The chord that becomes texture asks when harmonic identity gives way to mass. Pitch thickness asks the same question at the level of one tone. The structured tone asks how much internal order a timbre can carry. Pitch thickness names one dimension of that order. The pitch field asks how pitch behaves as a space rather than a point. Pitch thickness adds depth to the point itself.

Most usefully, pitch thickness gives generative music systems a low-level control that musicians might recognize. Text prompts can say "warm bass" or "aggressive lead," but those words hide several independent variables. A pitch-thickness control would let the system preserve the note while changing how many internal perceptual routes the note offers.

That is the compositional promise: do not treat pitch as the opposite of timbre. Treat pitch as a timbral structure that can be thinned, thickened, stretched, or made plastic.

A pitch can be one thing. It can also be a small, coherent crowd.
