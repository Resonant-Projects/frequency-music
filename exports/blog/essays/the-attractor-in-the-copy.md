## The Photocopier Test

There's an old thought experiment: photocopy a page, then photocopy the copy, then photocopy _that_. After enough generations, what survives? Not the text — that degrades into noise. What survives are the artifacts of the medium itself. Paper texture. Toner bloom. The machine's signature, amplified through iteration until it overwhelms the message.

A new TTS evaluation framework called I2D (Iterate to Differentiate) applies exactly this logic to voice synthesis. Take a TTS model's output, feed it back as the reference voice for a new synthesis pass, and repeat. Each iteration strips away what the model only approximated, leaving behind what it genuinely captured. Better models degrade more slowly. Worse models converge quickly to their attractor — the fixed point of their representation, which is usually a blurry, characterless average voice.

This is a technical contribution to speech evaluation. It's also, quietly, a profound statement about the nature of representation itself.

## Lucier's Protocol

Alvin Lucier understood this in 1969. "I Am Sitting in a Room" is the I2D protocol performed as art: record speech in a room, play it back through speakers in the same room, re-record, repeat. Over 32 generations, consonants dissolve, vowels blur, and what emerges are the resonant frequencies of the room — pure standing waves where the room's geometry reinforces certain pitches and cancels others.

Lucier's piece demonstrates what I2D measures: iteration reveals the attractor. The room's transfer function is the fixed point of the record-playback loop. Every pass strips away what the room doesn't reinforce, until nothing remains but resonance. The _medium_ speaks.

The I2D paper doesn't cite Lucier (why would it — it's a speech engineering paper), but the mathematical structure is identical. Both are iterated function systems where the fixed point reveals the channel's eigenstructure. Both prove quality through degradation rate. Both turn a copying process into an analysis tool.

## What the Attractor Tells You

Here's the deeper insight: every representation has an attractor, and **the attractor reveals what the representation actually captures versus what it only approximates.**

A TTS model's attractor is its "average voice" — the central tendency of its learned distribution. A room's attractor is its resonant modes. A tape machine's attractor is its frequency response curve plus noise floor. An MP3 codec's attractor is whatever survives below the perceptual masking threshold.

Each of these attractors has a different character:

- **Room resonance attractors** are harmonic (determined by geometry → standing wave ratios)
- **Tape generation loss attractors** are spectral (high frequencies die first, bias noise accumulates)
- **Codec attractors** are perceptual (shaped by psychoacoustic models of human hearing)
- **Neural model attractors** are statistical (the mean of the training distribution)

The rate of convergence to the attractor tells you how much information the channel carries versus how much it destroys. Lucier's room converges slowly because it reinforces specific frequencies — the channel has _structure_. A white noise generator would converge instantly because it has no memory. I2D's insight is that TTS quality correlates with convergence rate: better models are richer channels.

## The Plasticity Gap

The second paper — on detecting audio deepfakes — approaches the same territory from the opposite direction. Instead of asking "how does a system degrade under iteration?", it asks "how does a system _adapt_ under pressure?" Its answer: neuroplasticity-inspired algorithms that dynamically grow or shrink the number of active neurons in each layer during training.

The connection to I2D is subtler but real. A deepfake detector needs to distinguish between signals that have passed through a biological channel (a human vocal tract) and signals that have passed through a computational channel (a neural synthesizer). What it's really detecting is the _attractor signature_ — the artifacts that accumulate when a signal is generated by a particular system. Human voices carry the attractor of physical resonance (formants, breath noise, glottal pulses). Synthesized voices carry the attractor of their training distribution (spectral smoothness, averaged prosody, missing micro-variations).

Neuroplasticity helps because the relevant artifacts live at different scales for different deepfake methods. Some synthesizers smooth the spectral envelope (broadband artifact). Others produce micro-timing regularities (fine-grained artifact). A fixed architecture might be tuned to one scale but miss the other — echoing essay #76's argument that the granularity of attention determines what you can perceive.

## Composition as Iterated Channeling

This framework suggests a compositional practice: **design iterative processes whose attractors are musically interesting.**

Lucier showed the way. His room's attractor happened to be harmonically rich because rectangular rooms have standing wave frequencies in simple ratios. But this was somewhat lucky. A different room might produce an attractor that's a single muddy drone. The compositional art lies in choosing your channel wisely.

Consider what this means practically:

**1. Choose your channel for its attractor.** If you're going to run audio through an iterative process, the thing that matters most isn't the first pass — it's the fixed point. Record through a spring reverb ten times and you'll hear the spring's resonant modes. Record through a convolution reverb of a cathedral and you'll hear the cathedral's mode structure. The attractor _is_ the composition.

**2. Convergence rate is a parameter.** Lucier's piece works because convergence takes 32 iterations — slow enough that the listener hears the transformation as a process. If convergence happened in 2 iterations, the piece would be dull. If it took 200, the listener would lose patience. Compositional control over convergence rate (through channel design, gain staging, filtering) determines the temporal arc of the work.

**3. Fight the attractor for texture.** If iteration reveals what a channel preserves, then _resisting_ convergence creates tension. Inject new material partway through the iterative process. Modulate the channel itself between passes. Switch rooms mid-piece. The interplay between convergence toward the attractor and disruption away from it generates musical interest — analogous to harmonic tension and resolution.

**4. Layer attractors for complexity.** Run one signal through a room loop and another through a tape loop. Each converges toward its own attractor at its own rate. The composite texture is the interaction between two different fixed points — room resonance against tape saturation, harmonic modes against spectral rolloff. This is a form of counterpoint, but between processes rather than voices.

## The Copy Is Not the Original (And That's the Point)

The standard framing of signal degradation is negative: each copy loses information, fidelity decreases, the signal approaches noise. I2D adopts this framing — slower degradation means higher quality.

But Lucier's framing is the inverse: each copy reveals information _about the channel_. Degradation isn't loss — it's extraction. The copy isn't a worse version of the original. It's a portrait of the medium.

Both framings are mathematically identical. The difference is aesthetic. And it maps onto a fundamental choice in composition: do you treat your tools as transparent windows onto your intentions (minimize the channel, maximize fidelity), or as active participants whose character you want to amplify (maximize the channel, let the medium speak)?

The entire history of electronic and experimental music could be organized along this axis. Stockhausen's studio ideal: the channel should be invisible, pure realization of the score. Lucier's feedback ideal: the channel IS the score. Tape music, circuit bending, glitch, lo-fi — all practices that treat the attractor as the art.

## The Self-Decomposing Thread

This connects to essay #74's observation that sound is inherently self-decomposable — audio contains enough structure to separate its own components. I2D adds a temporal dimension to that claim: iterative processing doesn't just decompose a signal into components, it progressively reveals the channel's contribution versus the source's contribution. Given enough passes, the channel always wins. The medium always has the last word.

This is, in a sense, the acoustician's version of Marshall McLuhan: the medium is the message, and iteration is the proof.
